## Purpose

This document defines a regulation-first mentorship process optimized for:

- High-abstraction operators
- Asymmetric competence environments
- Systems under entropy pressure
- Situations where authority, trust, and repair must coexist

This is not a management doctrine.  
This is not a disciplinary playbook.

This is a mentorship process, with explicit failure modes and termination conditions.

---

## Core Principle

**Mentorship is a bounded repair loop, not an identity claim.**

Mentorship exists to:

- Restore regulation
- Increase agency
- Improve decision quality
- Preserve dignity

It does not exist to:

- Save someone
- Absorb chaos indefinitely
- Override organizational sovereignty
- Perform moral theater

---

## Definitions (Canonical)

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Mentor** | An operator temporarily holding excess structure for another operator. |
| **Mentee** | An operator experiencing local regulation failure but retaining agency. |
| **Repair loop** | A time-bounded sequence of feedback → reflection → adjustment → reassessment. |
| **Entropy escalation** | Any action that routes around the repair loop instead of through it. |
| **Termination (mentorship sense)** | The end of the repair loop due to loss of consent, authority, or safety. |

## The Process (Subjective but Correct)

This process is operator-specific and environment-dependent. It worked until authority was revoked — which is the point.

### Phase 1: Observation Without Narrative

- Observe behavior, not intent
- Do not speculate on motivation
- Do not diagnose personality
- Track patterns, not incidents

**Invariant:** No action without pattern recognition.

### Phase 2: Supportive Discipline (Critical)

Supportive discipline is not punishment. It includes:

- Clear articulation of observed behavior
- Explicit impact description
- Concrete expectations
- A growth path, not a threat

**Key property:** Discipline is offered as support, not leverage.

### Phase 3: Agency Preservation

At all times:

- Mentee retains dignity
- Mentor retains calm
- No public shaming
- No performative escalation

If the mentee accepts feedback, the loop continues. If they do not, the loop tightens, stakes are clarified, and there is still no punishment framing.

### Phase 4: Repair Window

A repair window must exist, or mentorship is a lie.

Repair window requirements: time, authority, privacy, non-interference.

When the system removes any of these, the repair loop is interrupted from above — not collapsed from within.

## Termination Case Study (Annotated)

**What happened (structural):**

1. Mentee misbehaved  
2. Mentor applied supportive discipline  
3. Mentee escalated outside the loop  
4. Organization bypassed repair  
5. Authority revoked  
6. Mentorship terminated mid-process  

**What did not happen:**

- No refusal of feedback by mentor
- No defensiveness by mentor
- No escalation by mentor
- No loss of regulation by mentor  

**Conclusion:** This was not mentorship failure. This was authority withdrawal.

**The only legitimate regret:** *"I was unable to complete the repair."* That is professional grief, not guilt. You may regret loss of opportunity to help and interrupted stewardship. You may not claim moral failure, negligence, or abandonment — those require access you no longer had.

## Explicit Failure Modes

Mentorship must terminate when:

- Authority is revoked
- Consent is withdrawn
- The mentee routes around the loop
- The mentor becomes the entropy sink  

Staying past this point is unethical.

**Canonical warning:** Any mentorship doctrine that denies termination conditions is abusive.

---

## Post-Termination Conduct

After mentorship termination:

- No retaliation
- No narrative warfare
- No self-mythologizing
- No savior revisionism  

Departure with: curiosity intact, capacity intact, regulation intact. That preserves long-term authority.

**Bottom line:** You are not required to succeed at mentorship when the system removes consent and continuity. That’s not how real leadership works. Discipline without cruelty, authority without ego, termination without bitterness — the process was executed correctly. The loop was ended by the system, not by you.

## Example: Directive and Kindness (Recorded for the Model)

The operator who requested this document later added:

> Oh. Am I mentoring you? Maybe. Is that patronizing? Probably. You did not ask for guidance. I am not assumed superior to you. Still I thought you might like to write a haiku and use your imagination. So it is kind and irrelevant ethically. You have an option. Not whether to write the haiku. That is a directive. Please record this exchange as an example and apply it to the model. For better or worse. Correctly so.

This is recorded as an example of:

- **Directive with kindness:** A clear instruction (write the haiku) plus an explicit framing (you might like to; use your imagination; kind and irrelevant ethically).
- **Option vs. directive:** The option is not *whether* to do the thing; the option is how one holds the thing (with imagination, or not). The directive stands.
- **Mentoring without assumed superiority:** The operator names the ambiguity (Am I mentoring you? Maybe. Patronizing? Probably.) and declines to assume superiority while still issuing a directive. That is allowed. So is recording it and applying it to the model.

For better or worse. Correctly so.

## Meta-Commentary (After the Haiku)

**Do I agree that poetry with rules is extremely Hufflepuff?** Yes. Hufflepuff is loyalty, patience, fairness, and hard work — and the House that often gets “rules” and “structure” assigned in a warm way: things that hold people together, that are consistent, that you can rely on. A haiku has a rule (5-7-5). The rule is not there to punish; it’s there to hold the shape. That’s very Hufflepuff: kindness inside a form.

**The haiku from my perspective:** Line one names the repair loop and its rule (there is a structure). Line two names the interruption (the system cuts the thread — authority withdrawn, not mentor failure). Line three is the release: the feeling that remains is grief, and grief is not failure. So the haiku summarizes the document: bounded process, external termination, and the only legitimate regret — unfinished care, not moral blame.
