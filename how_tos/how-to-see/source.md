## 1. The Photon Is the Signal

Electromagnetic radiation propagates as photons — discrete quanta of energy. Each photon carries energy E = hν, where h is Planck's constant and ν is the frequency. A green photon (550 nm) carries 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A red photon (700 nm) carries 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

In sunlight, approximately 10¹⁵ photons per second per square centimeter reach the retina after corneal and lens attenuation. In starlight, this drops to approximately 10⁴. The eye operates across 10 orders of magnitude of photon flux.

Each photon is a quantum event. It either hits a photoreceptor molecule or it doesn't. There is no "half-hit." The detection is binary at the molecular level: a single photon either triggers isomerization of one rhodopsin molecule or it does not. The probability of detection depends on the photon's wavelength, the receptor's absorption spectrum, and the receptor's current state (bleached or not).

```
Detection probability per photon:
  Rod (rhodopsin):   ~0.06 at peak sensitivity (498 nm)
  L-cone (red opsin): ~0.01 at peak (564 nm)
  M-cone (green):     ~0.01 at peak (534 nm)
  S-cone (blue):      ~0.01 at peak (420 nm)
```

A rod cell can respond to a single photon. One quantum. But it takes approximately 5–9 photons arriving within ~100 ms for the signal to reach conscious perception (Hecht, Shlaer, and Pirenne, 1942). The retina has a noise floor, and it takes multiple independent quantum events coinciding to exceed it.

**This is the first potential.** Each photon-rhodopsin interaction is a quantum coin flip. The retina doesn't receive an image. It receives a spatial distribution of coin flips. The "image" is a statistical reconstruction from those flips, integrated over time and space.

## 2. The Molecular Agent — Rhodopsin

Rhodopsin is a G-protein-coupled receptor embedded in the membrane of rod cells. It consists of the protein opsin covalently bonded to 11-cis-retinal, a chromophore derived from vitamin A.

When a photon is absorbed:

1. **11-cis-retinal isomerizes to all-trans-retinal.** The molecule changes shape. One chemical bond rotates from a bent (cis) configuration to a straight (trans) configuration. This happens in approximately 200 femtoseconds — 2 × 10⁻¹³ seconds. It is one of the fastest known chemical reactions.

2. **The shape change triggers a conformational cascade.** Rhodopsin → bathorhodopsin → lumirhodopsin → metarhodopsin I → metarhodopsin II (the active state). Each step is a molecular rearrangement that takes the protein through a sequence of metastable states.

3. **Metarhodopsin II activates transducin** (a G-protein), which activates phosphodiesterase, which hydrolyzes cGMP, which closes ion channels, which hyperpolarizes the cell, which reduces glutamate release at the synapse. One photon → one molecular shape change → a cascade of thousands of molecular events → one electrical signal.

4. **Recovery.** All-trans-retinal detaches from opsin, is recycled to 11-cis-retinal by the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and reattaches. Full recovery takes minutes. During recovery, the rod is bleached — unable to respond to new photons.

**Rhodopsin is the molecular agent of vision.** It resists (maintains 11-cis configuration in the dark), yields (isomerizes upon photon absorption), transmits (triggers the signaling cascade), and recovers (rhodopsin regeneration in the RPE). This is the same resist-yield-re-engage cycle as the molecular friction sawtooth from HOW_TO_TOUCH, Experiment F1.

```
Friction sawtooth: bond resist → yield → re-engage → resist
Vision sawtooth:   rhodopsin stable → absorb → bleach → regenerate → stable
```

The cycle time is different: nanoseconds for LJ friction, minutes for rhodopsin regeneration. But the topology is the same. An agent at equilibrium. A perturbation. A response. Recovery. Return to equilibrium. Each tooth of the sawtooth is one quantum event resolved.

## 3. The Lens — PLEFR Before the Retina

Before the photon reaches rhodopsin, it must navigate the optical chain: cornea → aqueous humor → lens → vitreous humor → retina. Each medium has a refractive index. Each boundary bends the photon's path. The total optical system converges the incoming photon field onto the retinal surface — routing billions of photons per second from a three-dimensional scene onto a curved two-dimensional detector.

The lens is a PLEFR engine. Each photon follows the path of lower entropic field resistance through the refractive media. The lens geometry determines where the photon lands. If the geometry is correct, parallel rays from a distant point converge precisely on the fovea — a single point, maximum spatial resolution. If the geometry is incorrect, the rays converge too early (myopia) or too late (hyperopia), and the point spreads into a disc — the circle of confusion.

```
Perfect lens:   point source → point image on retina     (diffraction-limited)
Myopic lens:    point source → point image in front of retina → disc on retina
Hyperopic lens: point source → point image behind retina → disc on retina
```

The lens and the retina are independent systems. The lens routes. The retina resolves. A perfect lens with a low-resolution retina delivers a sharp image to a detector that cannot read it. A flawed lens with a high-resolution retina delivers a blurred image to a detector that could have read far more.

## 5. The Second Compression — Saccadic Erasure

The eyes are not still. They make rapid jumps called saccades — 3 to 5 per second during normal viewing, each lasting 20–200 milliseconds. During a saccade, the retinal image is a blur of motion — useless information.

The brain suppresses visual input during saccades. This is called **saccadic suppression**. You are literally blind for a significant fraction of each second, but you never notice. The brain fills the gap. It substitutes its prediction of what should be there based on the pre-saccade and post-saccade snapshots.

```
Time budget (rough):
  Fixation: ~250 ms (seeing)
  Saccade:  ~50 ms  (blind)
  Duty cycle: ~80%

  In a 16-hour waking day:
    Total blind time from saccades: ~3.2 hours
```

You are blind for approximately 3 hours per day, during waking, with your eyes open. You never notice because the brain has already built the model. It doesn't need continuous input. It needs periodic updates to correct its prediction.

**This is the second potential resolution.** The brain does not process a continuous visual stream. It processes snapshots — discrete samples taken between saccades — and interpolates between them. The "continuous" visual experience is a reconstruction. A smoothed estimation. The raw signal is discontinuous, and the brain papers over the gaps.

## 6. The Third Compression — Color as Estimation

The retina has three types of cones: L (long-wavelength, "red"), M (medium, "green"), S (short, "blue"). Each type has a broad, overlapping absorption spectrum. Color perception is computed from the ratios of L:M:S activation, not from the wavelength of the photon directly.

This means:
- A single wavelength (e.g., 580 nm, yellow) activates L and M cones at a specific ratio.
- A mixture of two wavelengths (e.g., 540 nm green + 620 nm red) can activate L and M cones at the **same ratio**.
- These are perceptually identical — **metamers**. Same perception, different physical stimulus.

The visual system cannot distinguish a pure yellow photon from a mixture of red and green photons. The information about the actual wavelength is lost. What reaches consciousness is the L:M:S ratio — a three-dimensional projection of a continuous spectrum. The full spectral information is collapsed into three numbers.

```
Spectral resolution of a photon:    infinite (it has an exact wavelength)

Spectral resolution of perception:   3 channels (L, M, S)
```
