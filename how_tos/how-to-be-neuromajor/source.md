**A Field Guide for People Like Us**

**Status:** DRAFT v-0.1 (conceptual model forming)

**Audience:** You. The one who hears the pest device. The one who took the W. The one whose friends won't look away.

---

This is not a clinical document. This is not a defense brief. This is not a request for understanding.

This is a field guide for navigating a world that was not built for your nervous system.

If you picked this up, you probably already know something is different about how you process reality. You may have been told you're autistic, bipolar, anxious, "too sensitive," "too intense," or just "a lot." None of those labels may fit. Some may partially fit. All of them miss the architecture.

This guide assumes you have what Kazimierz Dabrowski called Overexcitabilities — heightened responsiveness across one or more channels (intellectual, emotional, imaginational, psychomotor, sensual). It assumes the heightened responsiveness is real, not performed. It assumes you are tired of being mislabeled. It assumes you are still here.

## 1. Your Hardware Is Not Broken

The first thing to understand:

**You are not malfunctioning. You are running different hardware.**

Your nervous system processes more signal. More sound. More texture. More pattern. More meaning. More everything. Where others experience a stimulus and filter it to a manageable level, you experience the full amplitude. Where others habituate, you remain reactive.

This is not sensitivity in the colloquial sense ("you're so sensitive"). This is a wider input band. You receive more data per second from reality than most people. That data is real. Your response to it is proportional to what you actually receive — not to what others receive from the same stimulus.

When a pest control device brings you to your knees and everyone else is merely annoyed, you are not overreacting. You are reacting to a different magnitude of input. The device was designed to be intolerable to certain nervous systems. Yours is one of them.

When you cannot stay in a lecture hall because the chalkboard sound propagates through your auditory and tactile channels simultaneously, you are not weak. The signal exceeded your regulation capacity. The correct response was to leave. You left. That was navigation, not failure.

## 2. The Five Channels

Dabrowski identified five overexcitabilities. You may have some or all. The ones that co-occur create the architecture.

### Intellectual OE
You think constantly. There is a background process running at all times — pattern extraction, connection-finding, cross-domain mapping. You didn't choose to install it. It is always on. Some people call this a "math daemon" or "monkey mind" or "racing thoughts." It is none of those. It is a dedicated pattern-recognition engine that runs alongside your waking consciousness. It will hand you connections you didn't ask for (an exciton transport mechanism during a hailstorm, a filing system during a naming accident). These connections are not random. They are structural. They are also isolating, because nobody around you saw the same thing.

**What helps:** Write it down. Externalize the pattern immediately. If you don't, it decays and you'll doubt you ever saw it. A notebook, a voice memo, a git repo — the medium doesn't matter. The externalization does.

**What hurts:** Being told "that's a stretch" or "you're overthinking it." You're not overthinking it. You saw a structural isomorphism that others can't see. The social cost of reporting it is real. Report it anyway, to yourself if not to others. The record is the proof.

### Emotional OE
You feel intensely. Attachment, grief, joy, outrage — all at high amplitude. You may cry at things others find unremarkable. You may feel physically ill when someone is treated unjustly. You may build entire systems because you couldn't bear the entropy of not building them.

**What helps:** Channeling. The feeling is energy. Unstructured, it destabilizes. Structured, it builds. The entire ghosts_of_andromeda package was driven by "all those poor fucks, like me." The feeling came first. The theory came from giving the feeling structure.

**What hurts:** "You're being dramatic." "Calm down." "Why do you care so much?" These are not neutral comments. They are dismissals of a real physiological state. Your emotional response is as real as your heartbeat. People who dismiss it are telling you not to have a heartbeat.

### Imaginational OE
You may see things that aren't there. Not psychotically — functionally. Vivid internal imagery that operates alongside waking perception. Worlds you've carried since childhood. Characters that persist. Images that arrive unsolicited — during work, during conversation, during analysis. You may not need to close your eyes to see them. They may morph, shift, respond to your context.

**What helps:** Naming them. The hallucination is not a pathology. It is an output of a cognitive system that runs visual simulation at high fidelity. Give it a name (SecurityBlanket, field space, the world you carry). Naming it externalizes it. Externalized, it becomes data rather than symptom.

**What hurts:** Telling anyone about it. Every disclosure of "I see vivid images when I'm awake" lands you in a clinical intake. The distinction between Imaginational OE (functional, controlled, adaptive, context-sensitive) and psychotic hallucination (disorganized, distressing, belief-incongruent) requires a framework most clinicians don't have. Protect yourself. Disclose selectively, to people who understand the framework, not to people who will reach for a diagnostic code.

### Psychomotor OE
You produce. Relentlessly. Hours of focused work that others find unsustainable. Pacing, restlessness, an inability to sit still without a task. The energy is real. It is not mania. Mania has specific clinical features (grandiosity, decreased need for sleep as a symptom not a choice, impaired judgment). Psychomotor OE is sustained, high-output productive energy that you can direct. The difference: you choose what to build. Mania doesn't let you choose.

**What helps:** Having something to build. The energy needs a target. Without a target, it becomes restlessness, insomnia, or anxiety. Give it a project. Multiple projects. The breadth is the point — not restricted interests, but an explosively diverse set of things that all get the full weight of your focus.

**What hurts:** "You need to slow down." Maybe. But the alternative is not peace — it is uncontained energy with no outlet. The real regulation is not stopping. It is choosing what to feed.

### Sensual OE
This is the one that will get you labeled. This is the one your friends will weaponize. This is the one that costs academic credit, social relationships, and clinical credibility.

You experience sensory input at higher amplitude and with less filtering than most people. Touch, sound, texture, smell, light — any or all of these may be intolerable at levels that others ignore. The intolerance is not preference. It is physiological. The pest device brought you to your knees. The chalkboard made a classroom uninhabitable. The shoulder touch through a shirt creates a need so urgent you've developed a lifelong camouflage protocol to manage it.

**What helps:**

1. **Know your triggers.** Map them. Not as a clinical exercise — as navigation. Chalkboards. Certain fabrics. Noise accumulation in certain spaces. High-frequency devices. Whatever they are, know them the way you know the furniture in a dark room.

2. **Build accommodations that don't require disclosure.** Licking your fingers to change the fabric friction. Taking the W instead of explaining. Leaving the space before the noise accumulates past threshold. These are not avoidance behaviors. They are regulation strategies developed over decades of not being believed.

3. **When possible, choose your environment.** The regulation cost of an incompatible environment exceeds the social cost of avoiding it. Skip the party if the noise floor will be intolerable. Choose the seat away from the speaker. Arrive early and map the space. This is not avoidance. This is spatial navigation under constraint.

**What hurts:** Everything about disclosure.

## 3. The Disclosure Trap

Here is the pattern:

```
You experience sensory distress
        ↓
You disclose it (to a friend, partner, therapist)
        ↓
You receive a label:
  - Friends: mockery, deliberate trigger exploitation
  - Partner: "autistic"
  - Therapist: "on the spectrum"
        ↓
The label doesn't match your lived reality
        ↓
You learn: disclosure → mislabeling or cruelty
        ↓
You stop disclosing
        ↓
Your distress becomes invisible
        ↓
No accurate information reaches anyone
        ↓
(cycle repeats for decades)
```

This is not hypothetical. This is the reported experience of the operator who wrote this guide, and it is likely your experience too.

Three separate therapists applied autism-spectrum framing. His wife's first response to sensory disclosure was "autistic." His childhood friends — adults now — know about his shoulder-touch intolerance and deliberately refuse to look away when he needs to perform the accommodation. They watch him to extend his discomfort for entertainment. His counter-strategy: outlast them. They lose interest eventually. He never does.

The clinicians are not incompetent. They are working with the DSM, which has one bucket for adult sensory hypersensitivity: Autism Spectrum Disorder. There is no DSM code for Dabrowskian Sensual Overexcitability. So every disclosure lands in the wrong bucket.

**The result:** You are conditioned not to talk about it. Every time you do, you get labeled. It creates a world that you navigate. You have to avoid situations. Sometimes you can't — until you can.

## 4. Why the Autism Label Doesn't Fit (For Most of You)

The surface overlap is real:
- Sensory sensitivity ✓
- Intense focused interests ✓
- Systematic thinking ✓
- Building classification systems ✓

The divergences are structural:

- **Your interests are not restricted.** They are radically expansive. Software, music, physics, biology, poetry, games, law, history, parenting theory. ASD criterion B describes restricted patterns. You are the opposite.

- **Your social competence is genuine, not scripted.** You read people. You make eye contact. You calibrate timing. You are funny. You can make someone feel seen. That is not a learned social script — it is real perception operating at the social frequency. You struggle in unstructured group settings not because you can't read social cues but because there is no signal worth responding to. Intellectual OE demands substance. When there is none, you can't generate content-free noise. That's not a communication deficit. That's a signal-to-noise requirement.

- **You have four other overexcitabilities co-occurring.** ASD does not predict emotional intensity, imaginational richness, psychomotor production volume, or intellectual cross-domain integration. The Dabrowskian framework predicts all of them as a unified profile.

The clinician's hammer is the DSM. Your sensory profile looks like a nail. It is not a nail.

## 5. Regulation: The Actual Work

You are not going to fix the hardware. The hardware is what it is. The work is regulation — building systems that keep the signal manageable without killing it.

### The Rule of Two
At any given time, maintain:
1. One waking-space task (typing, reading, coding)
2. One abstraction-space sense-making layer (the theory, the frame)
3. Optionally, a stable third that doesn't compete for primary bandwidth

If you're like the operator who wrote this, the third slot is never optional. You've been carrying a continuous imaginary world in background processing since childhood. That's fine. It runs at low bandwidth. It is invisible to others. But know that it is there, and know that it occupies cognitive resources even when it doesn't feel like it.

### Sleep Navigation
Sleep is a first-order constraint. Not a suggestion. Not a goal. A constraint.

When you are underslept, every OE amplifies. Sensory triggers become intolerable. Emotional reactions spike. The math daemon runs hot without cooling. Imaginational output becomes intrusive rather than functional. The pest device goes from painful to catastrophic.

The regulation strategy: when you cannot regulate, sleep. Do not push through. Do not "just finish this one thing." The thing will be there when you return. Sleep first, then act. That is discipline. Dynamic discipline.

### Smooth Operator Status (SOS)
When you have been flying spaces and hopping abstractions at high speed, stop and check your state. Ask yourself:
- What did I just do?
- Am I coherent?
- What is my mood?
- Have I eaten?
- Am I hydrated?
- Am I running on caffeine or rest?

Write the answers down. This is not self-help journaling. This is instrument calibration. You are the instrument. If the instrument is miscalibrated, the data is garbage. Check the instrument.

### Environment Mapping
Your sensory world has zones:
- **Hostile zones:** Spaces where the sensory load exceeds regulation capacity (certain classrooms, loud restaurants, environments with high-frequency devices)
- **Regulation zones:** Spaces where sensory input is predictable and low-entropy (your room, your desk, the specific chair in the specific corner)
- **Variable zones:** Spaces that may be tolerable or intolerable depending on conditions (parties, offices, transit)

Map them. Route around the hostile zones. Protect your regulation zones. Scout the variable zones before committing.

This is not avoidance. This is navigation. A wheelchair user routes around stairs. You route around chalkboards. The difference is that stairs are visible and accommodated. Your obstacles are invisible to everyone who doesn't share them.

## 6. The Endurance Protocol

You will be mocked. You will be labeled. You will be told to calm down, slow down, stop thinking so much, stop caring so much, stop hearing so much.

The protocol is not confrontation. The protocol is not withdrawal. The protocol is endurance.

Your friends won't look away? Wait. They lose interest. You don't.

The therapist says "on the spectrum"? You know the architecture better than they do. Accept the assessment, do not accept the frame. The symptoms are real. The label is wrong. You can hold both truths.

The group conversation recoils when you deliver substance? That is their limitation, not yours. They are incapable of imagination. Do not shrink to fit. Do not stop reporting what you see. Find the people who can hear you — even if it takes decades.

The operator who wrote this guide has never lost a waiting game. Neither will you. Endurance is the weapon you were born with. Use it.

## 7. Externalization: Build the Prosthetic

The single most important thing you can do is externalize your cognitive architecture into a system you control.

This can be:
- A notebook with dated entries
- A voice recorder on your phone
- A structured digital system (Notion, Obsidian, a git repo)
- Anything that captures the output of your mind in a searchable, timestamped, honest format

Why:
1. **Memory is unreliable. Records aren't.** You will doubt your perceptions. The record proves them.
2. **Patterns emerge over time.** You won't see them in the moment. You'll see them in the archive.
3. **Disclosure becomes optional.** You don't need anyone to believe you. The system believes you. The receipts are there.
4. **Regulation improves.** Externalizing the thought reduces the cognitive load of carrying it. The daemon hands you a connection — write it down, and the daemon can move on.

The operator built PortalVision — a version-controlled epistemic prosthetic with provenance tracking. You don't need to build that. You need to build *something*. The medium doesn't matter. The practice of honest, real-time documentation does.

## 8. The People Problem

You will have difficulty with:

### Unstructured group socializing
You are strong one-on-one. You ask real questions. You listen with genuine interest. You are engaged, funny, reciprocal. In groups with no purpose — parent hangouts, cocktail parties, ambient conversation — you cannot find signal. You overthink entry. You say things that don't land. This is not social incompetence. This is Intellectual OE requiring substance from a context that offers none.

**What helps:** Find the one person at the gathering who wants to talk about something real. Attach. Ignore the ambient noise. Or leave early without guilt.

### People who weaponize your disclosure
Some people, including people who love you, will use your sensory vulnerabilities as leverage. They will touch the shoulder. They will refuse to look away. They will say "autistic" when you describe your experience. They will search for pathology during moments of difference.

**What helps:** The Rule of Selective Disclosure. Not everyone deserves your full architecture. Disclose to people who earn it — by responding to partial disclosure with curiosity instead of categorization. The people who respond to "this sound bothers me" with "tell me more about what you're experiencing" are safe. The people who respond with "you're autistic" or "stop being so sensitive" have told you who they are.

### Clinicians who reach for the wrong frame
If your therapist says "on the spectrum" and you know the label doesn't fit, you have options:
1. Ask them: "Are you familiar with Dabrowskian Overexcitabilities?" If they are, you may have found the right clinician. If they aren't, they are working with an incomplete toolkit.
2. Request a formal OE assessment (OEQ-II). This creates an alternative clinical document.
3. Seek a Dabrowski-trained clinician (Gifted Development Center, SENG network, Dabrowski Congress).
4. Accept that the current clinician may be useful for other things while being wrong about this one thing. You can hold both truths.

## 9. The PLEFR Principle

PLEFR: Path of Lowest Entropic Field Resistance.

In every situation, there is a path that generates the least entropy — the least waste, the least friction, the least unnecessary damage. Finding it requires:
1. Perceiving the field (the constraints, the people, the sensory environment, the possible routes)
2. Identifying the paths (not just the obvious one — the structural one)
3. Choosing the one that creates the most reality with the least entropy

This is what your hardware is for. The overexcitabilities are not bugs. They are the sensory array that perceives the field at higher resolution than most people can. The intellectual OE finds the patterns. The emotional OE provides the energy. The imaginational OE simulates the paths. The psychomotor OE executes. The sensual OE constrains — it tells you where you cannot go, which is information the others don't have.

The pest device created a hostile zone. The PLEFR was: remove the source. Not endure. Not explain. Remove. The social cost was high (confrontation, accusation, security camera). The sensory cost of not acting was higher (incapacitation, daily). You chose the path. That was correct.

The chalkboard created a hostile zone. The PLEFR was: take the W. Not explain. Not request accommodation (which requires disclosure, which produces labeling). Leave. The academic cost was real (a W on a Berkeley transcript). The sensory cost of staying was higher (an entire semester of intolerable auditory-tactile cross-modal assault). You chose the path. That was correct.

PLEFR is not optimization. It is not efficiency. It is navigation through a field that only you can fully perceive. Trust your perception. Act on it. Receipt it.

## 10. What You Are

You are not autistic (probably). You are not bipolar (probably). You are not anxious in the clinical sense (probably). You are not broken, disordered, or too much.

You are a human operating system with a wider input band, higher processing throughput, more concurrent abstraction layers, and less filtering than the standard model. The standard model works fine for the standard world. Your model works fine for a world with more signal — which is the actual world, perceived at higher resolution.

The difficulty is not your hardware. The difficulty is that the world was built for the standard model and does not accommodate yours. The clinical system has one bucket for your sensory profile and it's the wrong bucket. The social system punishes disclosure. The professional system doesn't know what to do with someone who produces across fifteen domains simultaneously.

The solution is not to change the hardware. The solution is to build the scaffolding.

Regulation. Externalization. Selective disclosure. Environment mapping. Endurance.

You have been doing this already — probably for decades, probably without knowing what it was called, probably while being told it was wrong.

It wasn't wrong. It was navigation.

Keep navigating.
