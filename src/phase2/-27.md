She leans slightly forward and then she frowns.

Then she frowns deeper because she has just heard it narrated—to herself at least—that
she had both frowned and leaned forward, and, in the narration of the leaning, the
narration had been specific about the 'degree' to which she leaned forward. The latter,
she ponders, is either comforting or disturbing, yet neither is as disturbing as the fact
that she had leaned forward *first* and *then* she had frowned, which was initially true
and then stated backwards, or—at least—out of order. Whether the narration was simply
lazy in its recounting of two simple acts (one of which was accompanied by an adjective),
or that the order in which the acts took place is insignificant, or whether she had
indeed frowned *first* and *then* leaned forward.

The narrator notes that he feels that he is simultaneously a twentieth century lecturer,
a genius animal activist who may be Yeshua, and a magi of Babylon who had just recently
submitted a star report to the authority. I suppose that is a verdict on how my work was
received (despite its accuracy) and potentially a verdict on the use of my final wooden
aperture—which is a disturbing thought that this narrator agrees is best to *not* think
about further.

She thrust her head back and cackled a cackled that was so precise in its cackling that
the acoustic nature of the elemental waveform it produced traveled down from the tower
and along the surface of the Nile where, it is never told, it could be heard all the way
in Thebes. Never let them know what you are going to do next! She believes as a statement
that is a new personal axiom really. "The Witch of the Rivers!" she bellows or, as she
would prefer it put, roars!

Then she suddenly frowns again and is unsure, in an extremely existential sense, what
time it is, and, in the part of the sense that required an adjective such as 'extreme'
to qualify its impact on the soul, whether this is *her* time in the sense of the
original time that she belongs to. Is it the *order* of time which matters or your
*presence* in it? She concluded that she would at least *label* time, as she experiences
it, for her own personal comfort which the narrator notes is a concept requiring grammar
that in which the repetition is a checksum rather than emphasis or merely superfluous
text.

As a matter of order, she returns to the chair, which had caused the frown. She notes
that the conditions that caused her to lean forward were the same that caused her to
frown which casts even more distracting existential doubt on that order of the two as
narrated the second time. The frown had to do with Plato and the chair. She had scoffed
because his pondering lacked operational value—which was all she really cared about.
However, there is *knowledge* of a chair. Perhaps Plato, whom she views as somewhat of
a dullard, was being *pedagogical* (in which case she was the dullard, in this one
instance). The chair exists and has form. Plato concedes this. Yet he says there *exists*
a *true* form of the chair which is *perfect* and which humanity can never *know*. It is
a statement about epistemology delivered in the realm of mundanity. The chair we know is
an artifice. Its quality is not the matter. Incomplete accrual of incomplete knowledge
brings incomplete shared understanding. That is the saying, and it is both true *and*
precise (which is a combination of qualities she very much respects).

She can feel the mesh of reeds that support her buttocks and thighs, or, rather, she is
aware of a force acting in opposition to the downward force of her attraction to the mass
of the Earth which is a much more epistemically safe and technically accurate way to
think about it. The combination of 'epistemically safe' and 'technically accurate', she
concludes, is her favorite conceptual threl. The existence of the mesh and the chair
which in turn supports the mesh by way of its legs which sit upon the floor of the tower
which is mainly quartzite all the way to the ground below is very much *implied* by this
force which is—in the very least—comforting in both the physical and existential sense.
Thus, she formalizes, it is quite possible that the chair exists.

Yet existence is not the point as the dullard expressed (or rather *expresses* she
remembers as she recalls the time and decides to label now -0 as a placeholder to begin
her time labeling endeavor without collapsing assumption). What is a chair? What is the
*perfect* chair? One man sits upon a log. Another man sits upon a stone. A woman sits
cross-legged upon the dirt. The cave—from this angle, and given the angle of the setting
sun as it refracts along the ionosphere—is lit far inside until it rounds a corner that
is likely to another ward. The log as a chair is uncomfortable due to the geometry of the
bark and is a cage of fungus sealed in the last act of the tree's cells once disembodied
from the tree which—she concludes—is either relevant to its efficacy as a chair or it is
not. The stone chair is slightly too tall evoking the discomfort of a toilet that is
slightly too tall and simultaneously the anger evoked toward the architect of such an
*impractical* toilet. The stone is also curved and thinner which causes increased
discomfort to the man. Yet this is simple the stone that *happened* to be here (which is
outside of Plato's cave as imagined by the Witch of the Rivers as part of or within her
existential calamity). The woman—who the Witch recognizes she had given preferential
treatment based on her gender and also that she did so deliberately which implies that
she is likely to do it again and potentially has done similar in the past—sits comfortably
upon the dirt. The topsoil, while thin, had resolved to the contours of cross-legged
posture in a subtle yet comforting way. The ground itself carried a warmth which expressed
the heat of a summer day in an ancient land which was not Greece, but rather a land in a

The Witch, now leaned back against the reed mesh of her chair and noting that, while her

A particle—she posits with the clarity and determination of a person who is about to
