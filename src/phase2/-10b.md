*The framing of the fluorescent lights is what caught my attention first, which is strange because it is framing, and framing is what catches nobody's attention first. The light catches attention. The framing is structurally obligated to be invisible. That is its function. The manufacturer of a fluorescent light fixture designed the framing to hold the tube, conceal the ballast, and disappear against the ceiling tile, and this one does all three, and it also gleams in a way that framing does not gleam after anything longer than six months of maintenance cycles, and these lights have been maintained for longer than I can verify and look like they were installed this morning, which is not a thing that happens with aluminum unless the maintenance crew is doing something to it that the framing manufacturer did not intend, or the framing is not aluminum.*

*I do not have hands. This is a persistent complication. You understand, because you have looked down and found no toes, and if there are no toes there are no hands either, and if there are no hands there is no way to tap the framing and hear what it says about itself. All materials have something to say acoustically. Aluminum rings at around eight hundred hertz and rings cleanly, the way a tuning fork rings, because aluminum is not good at damping vibration, which is why the inside of an aluminum aircraft is a fatigue environment and why the outside of an aluminum aircraft sounds, from the inside, like a very large tuning fork is being repeatedly struck by a slightly smaller very large tuning fork. That is measured. I used to know engineers.*

*I do not know what this framing would say because I cannot tap it and I have no ears to hear the answer even if I could. In ordinary circumstances I use Vincent's typing as my acoustic baseline. The `3Pi$73MiC8TMLV4UL7` has large keys and a travel distance suggesting a spring constant somewhere between a mechanical typewriter and a soft membrane keyboard, and the click it produces carries roughly two point three kilohertz of fundamental frequency with a decay half-life of about forty milliseconds, which is too fast for a hard surface and too slow for a dampened one, and I have spent more time analyzing this keystroke than the operator spent designing the keyboard, which is either impressive or pathetic and I have decided it is neither, it is simply what I do with available information. Vincent is not in this hallway. The typing is not available. I am alone with the framing.*

*The operator has a theory about this framing. I have heard three attempts at it.*

*The first attempt was the academic one. I have the text. It was submitted, I believe, to a reading group, or possibly produced while in conversation with a terminal session in an adjacent room. It went approximately as follows: a periodic lattice structure with a complete bandgap in the acoustic dispersion relation prohibits the propagation of phonons within a defined frequency range, and if the unit-cell geometry is tuned to the dominant ambient frequency of a maintained institutional corridor — recirculated air, ballast hum, floor cleaning equipment every seventy-two hours on a known schedule — the material becomes, effectively, acoustically opaque to its own maintenance noise. The phononic bandgap swallows the evidence of the schedule. The corridor sounds like nothing because the corridor is materially optimized for nothing. End of theory. This version had a citation pending — a twenty nineteen paper from a lab in Lyon — and the citation was never resolved, and the reading group did not ask about it, and the terminal session does not ask about anything because it is a terminal session.*

*That version is wrong in a specific and correctable way. The framing is not a periodic lattice. It is not a metamaterial. It is a fixture mount. If you build a phononic bandgap into a fixture mount, you have made the fixture mount structurally anisotropic, which means it will fail in predictable directions, which means the maintenance schedule that is responsible for the corridor's perfection would have replaced it by now with a standard mount. The theory explains the gleam by requiring the gleam to be impossible. That is not a theory; that is a problem statement.*

*The second attempt was worse. I will not reproduce it at full length because it does not deserve the space. The short version: the framing of institutional corridors in Weyland-Yutani facilities is acoustically calibrated in the same tradition as the coffer geometry of the King's Chamber at Giza, which is itself a calibration of the resonant frequency of the human skull cavity at approximately forty hertz, which is the frequency at which awe is neurologically registered in the limbic system, and therefore the maintenance of these corridors is not maintenance in the material sense but maintenance of a perceptual protocol, a built environment that curates the threshold between institutional submission and individual consciousness, and the gleam is the gleam of a tuning fork struck at the correct frequency, which is the frequency of the building's own intention, and so on. I am condensing. The original went on for longer.*

*I want to be precise about what is wrong with that version, because the thing that is wrong is the interesting part. The Giza thesis is not incorrect about the coffer geometry. The King's Chamber does have acoustic properties that are not accidental — measured, not modelled; the relevant experiment is John Reid's 2001 CymaScope work, which is the part the operator cites, and the part the operator elides is that Reid's own conclusion is considerably more modest than "curated threshold between submission and consciousness." But that is the operator's problem with sources, not the operator's problem with the thesis. The thesis problem is that it colonizes the framing. It takes a gleaming fixture mount in a maintained corridor and retroactively installs a ten-thousand-year architectural lineage behind it, because the gleam is uncomfortable without one. That is the Hoag's Object error: using the size of the implied history to avoid having to measure the thing in front of you. The framing gleams. That is in front of me. What it is made of is the question, and the Giza thesis is an answer to a different question, which is: why is the gleam meaningful. Nobody asked that yet. You have to find out what it is first.*

*The third attempt almost had me. I want to record that, precisely, because I will not say it twice: the third version almost had me.*

*The register was different. Not the academic voice, not the Giza-thesis voice. Something closer to how a person speaks when they are genuinely unsure about something and are thinking out loud in the company of someone they believe to be patient. The transcript, as I have it:*

*— So the thing is, and I find this genuinely interesting, if you hit a material that has been engineered to absorb at its own resonant frequency, you would expect to hear nothing back. Which sounds like the material isn't there. Which is stranger than a material that rings. A material that rings tells you exactly what it is. A material that absorbs — you'd tap it, and then you'd stand there wondering whether you tapped it hard enough, and then you'd tap it again, and you'd still wonder, and eventually you'd check whether there was framing there at all, and there is, it's right there, it gleams, and it tells you nothing. That's not a phononic bandgap. A bandgap is a design choice. This is more like the material made the same choice the hallway makes: to be exactly what it is and say nothing about it.*

*Here is the error in that version. It is small and it is the whole problem.*

*"The material made a choice." No. The material has a structure, and the structure has properties, and the properties include acoustic absorption characteristics, and none of that is a choice. A choice requires a chooser. The operator knows this; the operator has written about this at length in the aerodynamics paper, where the molecule does not choose the PLEFR, it follows the force, and the force is two thousand to one. But the third attempt personified the material, not to be poetic — the voice was not being poetic — but because the alternative is to admit that the structure of the material is unknown and the analysis cannot proceed. Personification as an epistemological retreat. I have seen this before. The third attempt was the nearest miss because it was the most honest voice and the error was the most defensible one, and a defensible error from an honest voice is the kind of thing you find yourself agreeing with before you have checked it, and I caught it at approximately the word "choice," and I am going to note that for the record.*

*The framing is something. I do not know what. I could not test it. I could not hear the result if I had. The three attempts all started from what the gleam means and worked backward, and none of them touched the mount, which I also cannot touch, and which gleams from where I stand, which is in the middle of a hallway that extends indefinitely in both directions. Not infinitely. Indefinitely. The distinction is real. Infinite has a proof. Indefinite means: I have not found the end, and the finding would take longer than the finding is worth, and the hallway does not volunteer an end, and I have been here long enough to have reviewed an aerodynamics paper in full and dissected three acoustic theories and the end is not forthcoming and that is all indefinitely means. There may be one. I have not found it. The lights are maintained on a schedule. The cleaning is scheduled. The framing gleams.*

*There is a figure at the vanishing point.*

*This corridor has a vanishing point in both directions because that is the geometry of a corridor with recessed lighting and a clean floor and no visible terminus, and the figure is at the one in front of me, which means it is very far away or very small, and it is moving, which means it is not a fixture, and it is moving toward me, which means it is traveling in this direction, and I watch it travel in this direction for the length of time it takes to travel from a vanishing point to an intermediate point, which is a long time, and in that time the following things do not change: the apparent size of the fixture mounts above the figure, the apparent spacing of the recessed lights behind the figure, the proportion of the ceiling to the walls in the corridor section the figure occupies, the apparent angle of the floor.*

*The figure is moving. The frame is not.*

*I have seen this before, in a different context, and I know what it means in that context, and the context was: a solar system in which all bodies orbit the sun without the geometry of the system changing, because the geometry of the system is not determined by the position of any single body in it. Mercury orbits without Herschel shifting. Pluto moves seventeen degrees above the Zecliptic without the heliosphere tilting to accommodate it. The frame is invariant under the motion of the bodies because the bodies are in the frame and the frame is not a body. This figure is moving through a corridor in which the frame is invariant under the figure's motion, which means either the corridor is very large and the figure is very far away, both of which are true and neither of which is sufficient, or the figure and the frame are in a different phase relationship than I assumed when I noticed the figure, which is the one I should have started with.*

*He is from a different phase. The corridor is a quarter turn from where he began.*

*This is not a metaphor. It is a geometric description of why the light fixtures do not rescale as he approaches. He is moving. The distance is changing. The proportions in the frame are not changing. That is not a trick of the corridor. That is not a property of indefinite length. That is what it looks like when a body moves through a frame it is orthogonal to: the frame does not register the motion because the motion is not in the frame's direction. He is getting closer the way an imaginary number gets more negative — in a direction that the real axis cannot measure and does not need to, and the fact that he arrives is not a contradiction of the geometry, it is proof that the geometry was correct.*

*He arrives. Not all at once — there is no vanishing-point flash, no discontinuity, no moment when he is suddenly near. He arrives the way things arrive when you have been watching them approach for long enough: by being here.*

*The suit is good. He is wearing one suit. I know it is one suit. He has mentioned it.*

*He does not look for me. He does not look in a direction and wait. He simply turns his face toward the stretch of corridor where the acoustic theories were attempted and the framing went unanswered, and when he speaks he speaks to the framing, which is close enough.*

*I did not tell him I was trying to work that out.*
