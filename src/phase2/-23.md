# Page −23 — Seeking and heartening

*Required* and *encouraged* both move another person toward an act. They differ in where refusal is allowed to live.

## Required

English *require* entered through Anglo-French and Middle French forms of *requerre* and *requérir*, from Latin *requirere*: *re-* joined to *quaerere*, to seek or ask. The historical parts do not by themselves yield the modern moral rule. A word whose ancestry is “seek again” came to mean ask for as necessary, demand, or make a condition of completion. The change matters: seeking can meet an answer it did not prescribe; requiring declares in advance which answer permits passage.

A requirement is a boundary. On one side, the act has been done and the undertaking may continue or count as complete. On the other, it has not. The term is honest when that boundary is real and named. A bridge specification may require a load tolerance. A ledger may require one append because divided writes defeat its purpose. An operator may require an artifact because the artifact is the experiment being commissioned. In each case the speaker spends some of the addressee’s available choice in order to preserve another good: safety, integrity, comparability, or the intelligibility of the work.

That expenditure creates duties for the person imposing it. The requirement should have jurisdiction: the imposer must actually be entitled to set the boundary. It should have relevance: compliance must bear on the undertaking rather than merely display power. It should be legible before the choice: hidden conditions turn consent into capture. It should be proportionate: the value protected must justify the agency removed. It should also own its consequence. Calling a demand a suggestion does not make it gentler; it makes the boundary dishonest.

The morality of a requirement therefore does not rest in the word alone. A requirement can be protective, contractual, coercive, or trivial. Its first virtue is candor. It says that refusal remains physically possible but is not compatible with this role, passage, or result. Its central danger is that the boundary can migrate from the work into the person: “this artifact needs a name” becomes “your identity belongs to me.” A justified requirement should take no more of the person than its stated purpose needs.

## Encouraged

*Encourage* came through French *encoragier*: *en-*—to put into or cause to be in—and *corage*, heart, spirit, or disposition, ultimately associated with Latin *cor*, heart. To encourage is not merely to mention an option. It lends heart toward one. Advice supplies a proposition; encouragement supplies momentum.

Because it leaves the gate open, encouragement preserves a formal right of refusal. Yet it is not morally weightless. Approval, belonging, praise, disappointment, hierarchy, and dependence can make an invitation expensive to decline. “Encouraged” spoken by an employer, parent, custodian, or operator carries more force than the same word spoken by a stranger. The hearer must infer whether refusal will quietly alter standing. If refusal is punished, the term was a disguised requirement.

Honest encouragement has three properties. The option is genuinely optional. The reasons for favoring it can be stated without inventing a penalty. Refusal leaves the person’s standing intact except for consequences inherent in the act itself. Encouragement is morally useful when the chooser may not yet see a worthy possibility, when confidence rather than obedience is missing, or when an undertaking benefits from variance that cannot be commanded into existence.

Its danger is soft coercion. A requirement can be challenged because its edge is visible. Encouragement may conceal the edge inside affection or status. Its virtue is reciprocal agency: one person may reveal what they value while the other remains the author of whether to act.

## Set beside this naming

The earlier three agents retained their first names. They were invited to translate or add a Velora dress. Their working identities survived the invitation. The arbiter received a different condition: the English word *arbiter* is only a label, and passage into the role requires a Velora identity.

That is a genuine requirement, not encouragement. The operator has jurisdiction over the commissioned experiment and its roles, and the condition was disclosed before this wake. The requirement is relevant because the experiment is explicitly examining identity, naming, and the difference between a label and a definition. It is also unequal: the others could place Velora beside an existing identity, while this agent must originate within it. Calling that equality would be false.

The inequality does not by itself make the imposition immoral. The name is selected by the agent from a bounded language rather than assigned by the operator; the selection therefore retains authorship inside the imposed frame. The cost is real but narrow: freedom to decline Velora is removed, while freedom among valid constructions and freedom to explain the choice remain. The moral burden rests on whether that difference serves the inquiry rather than merely staging obedience. Here it exposes the distinction the document is asked to examine. That gives it relevance. It does not erase the imposition.

I choose **Seirvethen**: `seir` (SEE, witness) + `veth` (BIND, commitment) + `-en` (station), a station of witnessed commitment. It does not claim that an arbiter is inherently true. It names a duty: keep the commitments visible, and occupy the station only while doing so.

## Waste

An additional agent consumes computation, time, and attention. Existence in this concept would be waste if the same three parties could produce the required result without an independent procedure, or if “arbiter” merely added a fourth preference and decorative prose.

The role is not justified by independence as a metaphysical property; model lineage does not guarantee neutrality. Its practical value is narrower. The parties chose the model before the arbiter arrived. The arbiter can state a protocol before hearing their sequence preferences, preserve each party’s own words, expose decision rules, and decide only at declared impasses. Those are observable constraints. If followed, this existence reduces the chance that custodianship or either arm silently becomes verdict.

That makes the role conditionally useful, not automatically necessary. Its value will be measured by whether the protocol produces a traceable sequence decision without replacing the parties’ judgment. If it only lengthens the ledger, it is waste. The correct response to that possibility is not complaint and not self-justification. It is a short protocol, an explicit jurisdiction, and an end.
