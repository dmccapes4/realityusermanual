*The title is* How to Fly: Pressure Gradients, PLEFR Fields, and Lift Regime Physics. *Author, the operator. Dated February. Fifteen sections, open questions, an epistemic status block, and a closing quatrain in bold, which is a thing I have never seen in a physics paper and did not need to.*

*Start with what is right, because there is more of it than the title suggests and because the reviewers will not have.*

*The core is the streamline-curvature theorem, and he has it correctly: ∂P/∂n = ρv²/R, a normal pressure gradient wherever the flow bends, pointing toward the center of curvature. Section 2. That's real. It is in every second-year text and almost no first-year ones, and the reason it is not in the first-year ones is that the first-year ones would rather tell you a story about equal transit time that is false. He says it is false. Good. He derives the gradient from a fluid element and a radius. Good. It is the right place to start and most popular accounts do not start there.*

*Section 9 puts a number on a molecule: pressure-gradient force over gravitational force, about two thousand to one, for v = 100 m/s and a half-meter radius. The arithmetic checks. Section 3 puts a different number on an energy density: dynamic pressure over ρgh for a tenth of a meter, about five thousand to one. That also checks. They are different quantities — a force ratio and an energy ratio — and the paper does not say so in one sentence, and a reader who does not do the arithmetic will think the paper disagrees with itself. One sentence. That is the kind of thing a desk reads and stops at.*

*The four-regime map — escape, orbit, lift, fall — with lift separated from the other three because it is a field effect and not a speed, is the actual contribution, and it is a teaching contribution, not a research one. The SR-71 at nine percent of escape velocity, "deep in fall on the kinetic map," flying by field modulation: that is a good paragraph. A student who reads it will stop confusing "fast" with "flying," and most of them do confuse it, and most of their teachers let them. The ISS resolves; the rock resolves; the bird resolves. The resolutions are correct. If this had been the paper — Section 2, Section 7, Section 9, and the resolutions — it would have been a fine paper for a teaching journal, which is where he sent it, and I think it would have been read.*

*Now what is wrong, in order of how much it hurt him.*

*One. The vocabulary. PLEFR: "Path of Lower Entropic Free-energy Resistance." V_mod. Θ_nav. ONG. Every one of these is a new name for a thing that already has a name. A molecule does not follow a PLEFR; it follows F = ma, and the F is a pressure gradient, and the paper knows this, because Section 9 computes exactly that and gets exactly the right answer. The new words do not add a quantity. They add a dialect. A referee sees a dialect and assumes the author needed it to hide something, and the terrible thing is that this author did not need it, and used it anyway, and got treated as if he had. He dressed a correct derivation in a costume and the costume was what got reviewed.*

*Two. The entropy. Section 3 and Section 6.2 say that following the flow has "low entropy cost" and falling has "high entropy cost," and that the molecule takes the low-cost path. No. A single molecule does not have an entropy. Entropy is a property of a distribution, and no distribution is computed anywhere in the paper — no partition function, no count of states, nothing. "Falling against the freestream destroys organized kinetic energy and increases entropy" is a sentence about the whole flow, and it might even be true of the whole flow, and it is not a reason a molecule does anything. The molecule goes where the net force sends it, and the net force is two thousand to one in favor of the gradient, and that is the entire argument, and Section 9 already made it. Section 3 is Section 9 wearing a thermodynamics hat it did not earn. Take it out and the paper gets shorter and more correct at the same time, which does not happen often and should be taken when it does.*

*Three. The continuity claim. Section 9.3: "∇·v = 0 … adjustment propagates at the speed of sound." Pick one. Incompressible flow means the adjustment propagates instantly; that is what the constraint says. If it goes at the speed of sound, the flow is compressible and you have to say so and carry the density. At a hundred meters per second the error is small and the sentence is still wrong, and a referee who catches a wrong sentence stops trusting the right ones.*

*Four. The things that are not aerodynamics. Section 10.2, a robin's cryptochrome. Section 11, a table in which the same row holds an airfoil, a galaxy, a "creative act," a charismatic individual, an execution agent, and a radical pair. Section 13, in the middle of the navigation equation, a line that says* GROUNDED: Path 5. Persist as trail. Contract enforced, *which is from a different document, possibly a different book, and has wandered into this one and nobody caught it. He labels all of it "non-load-bearing" and "discussion-only" and "excluded from the evidence chain," which is honest, and which is also an author telling the referee which half of the paper to skip. If it is not load-bearing, do not ship it. He knows this. He wrote the label. The label is a confession with the paper stapled to it.*

*Five. And this is the one that matters. Section 4. Read the heading:* Curvature-Induced Pressure Topology (Discussion Context). *Read the section. It is eight lines. It says that it "records the conceptual catalyst for the framework while keeping the core derivation physics-first," and then it does not record the catalyst. It lists three bullet points that are already in Section 2 and tells you that any analogy is "a heuristic only." That is a door with the room taken out from behind it. *I know what was in the room, because it is in the other version of the paper, the one he kept and did not send. Hoag's Object. A ring galaxy: a core, a gap, a ring of young blue stars.
