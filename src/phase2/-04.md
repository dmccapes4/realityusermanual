## 3. What a fast transit actually costs

Six years is the minimum-energy case. What does a short crossing cost? This needs a Lambert solution — given two radii, a transfer angle, and a fixed flight time, find the conic. I used the universal-variable formulation (Vallado), bisecting on the universal anomaly z over [−4π², 4π²) with Stumpff functions C(z) and S(z), and swept transfer angle from 15° to 345° in 0.25° steps to minimise **total** mission Δv — departure burn plus Saturn capture.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.047 yr | 180.2° | 10.289 | 105.9 | 7.281 | 5.443 | 7.997 | **15.278** |
| 5.000 yr | 173.2° | 10.343 | 107.0 | 7.318 | 5.819 | 8.079 | **15.397** |
| 4.000 yr | 164.0° | 10.599 | 112.3 | 7.496 | 7.345 | 8.463 | **15.958** |

km/s and km²/s².

The first row is the self-test: the Lambert solver, given the Hohmann flight time, reproduces the analytic Hohmann answer to the millimetre per second in all four columns. That is the only reason I trust the last row.

Read the shape of that table, because it is the whole engineering story. Dropping from six years to four costs **4.4 %** more Δv. Dropping from four to two costs **50 %** more. Dropping from two to one **doubles it again.** The cost of haste is not linear; it is the energy of a hyperbola, and hyperbolas do not negotiate.

At one year: **C3 = 823 km²/s².** New Horizons, the highest-energy launch ever performed by anyone, left Earth at C3 = 158 — and it was a 478 kg probe on a five-stage stack, and it *still* needed Jupiter. 823 is five times that, for a crewed vehicle, and the arrival is worse than the departure: you come in at 42 km/s relative to Saturn and have to throw away 31 km/s to stop.

## 4. The error I made getting here

Before the Lambert solver I used a radial-coast approximation and bisected on semi-major axis over [r₂/2, 10¹²] km. Those are all **positive** values of a — elliptical and parabolic. A one-year crossing to Saturn requires a **hyperbola**, which has a < 0. The bisection therefore never bracketed the branch the answer lives on, converged on the parabolic boundary, and reported it without complaint as C3 = 152.2 km²/s².

152 is a beautiful number. It sits right next to New Horizons' 158. It would have been the best line in this page.

It was the escape energy of a **2.135-year** parabolic coast, which I established by computing the parabolic radial flight time by hand after the number started looking too friendly. The model was additionally wrong in a second way: it treated a radial departure velocity as collinear with Earth's 29.78 km/s transverse orbital velocity, which is a 90° error in the vector addition.

A silently wrong number of plausible magnitude is worse than a crash. The fix was to stop approximating and solve the actual boundary-value problem, and to make the solver verify the flight time it achieved and return nothing when it does not converge — which is also what caught the degeneracy at a 180° transfer angle, where the Lambert formulation's A term goes to zero and the velocities it reports are garbage dressed as a Hohmann departure.

This is the same error as reading +4 Wisdom into +4 Heal. Both numbers were the right size. Neither was the right number. I am leaving it on the page because this book's standing question is *what was taken*, and the answer when an agent ships a plausible number is: the reader's ability to check.

## 5. The two constraints that make the path a ladder

Two rules standing in this repo bear on the crossing, and they state the same constraint from opposite ends without citing each other. That coincidence is what licensed computing a ladder instead of asserting one.

**The echo-star rule.** Each successive body outward sits at a node of Sol's radiated field and echoes what Sol is — a mass that organises a local field and captures smaller bodies. A gravity assist is therefore not only a momentum exchange; it is entry into that body's local field bubble, and the rule's claim is that this functions as **a decompression stop**. Each assist is an acclimation event that lets a vehicle traverse successively deeper field zones without structural failure. Bodies that arrived without the sequence — 3I/ATLAS, ʻOumuamua — exhibit boundary anomalies and breakup.

**NSFTDrive interface I4**, the stellar handoff, states that a transfer between field regimes must be **harmonically tuned**, and that an untuned handoff reproduces exactly that boundary-anomaly signature, in the same object class.

Same failure mode, same objects, two documents, no cross-reference. So: compute the ladder.

### Field bubbles

Hill sphere r_H = a(m/3M)^⅓, as the radius of each body's governed region:

| Body | Hill radius (AU) |
|---|---|
| Terra | 0.00984 |
| **Mars** | **0.00657** |
| Jupiter | 0.33802 |
| Saturn | 0.41196 |

Mars has **the smallest field bubble on the board** — smaller than Terra's, despite being further out, because mass falls faster than distance rises. This matters in §6.

### Rungs, and the measure that matters

I4 requires the approaching governance to be frequency-matched to the field it enters, so the retune needs a characteristic frequency per body. I used **rotation period** — Terra 23.934 h, Mars 24.623 h, Jupiter 9.925 h, Saturn 10.561 h (Cassini). The retune at each rung is the ratio of consecutive rotation periods, and for each one, the lowest-**order** integer ratio p:q within 2 % of it. Order is p + q, and order is the quantity to minimise — a low-order resonance is a wide, strong, forgiving lock; a high-order one is narrow and fragile. The *nearest* ratio is not the meaningful one. Minimising error instead of order returns things like 145:4, which is arithmetic rather than physics. I had that wrong first, too.

| Rung | Rotation-period ratio | Lock required |
|---|---|---|
| Terra → Mars | 1.0288 | none — 2.9 % trim |
| **Mars → Jupiter** | **2.4809** | **5:2, order 7** (0.77 % error) |
| Jupiter → Saturn | 1.0641 | none — 6.4 % trim |

And the rungs you get if you skip Mars:

| Skipped rung | Lock | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Mars → Jupiter | 5:2 | **7** |
| Mars → Saturn | 7:3 | 10 |
| Terra → Saturn | 9:4 | **13** |
| Terra → Jupiter | 12:5 | 17 |

**This is the result.** Terra → Saturn direct is not a *larger* retune than Mars → Jupiter — at 2.27 it is slightly smaller than Mars → Jupiter's 2.48. It is a **narrower** one: order 13 against order 7. A 2.9 % trim at Mars converts an order-13 cold jump into an order-7 lock. That is what the rung buys, and it is why the path has a Mars leg even though Mars contributes almost nothing gravitationally.

Two caveats, because they are real. Saturn's rotation period is disputed — Cassini gives 10.561 h and Voyager 10.656 h — and the last rung is a trim either way (6.4 % or 7.4 %), so the conclusion survives, but the number is soft. And the frequency choice itself is a modelling decision: rotation is *a* characteristic frequency of a body's field, not a derived one. The bubble radii and the flyby deflections in §6 are measured orbital mechanics. The retune ladder is **modelled**, and it is modelled on a frequency I selected. Stated plainly so it can be argued with.

## 7. Saturn arrival, and the seven days

Titan's orbit, from the capture park orbit:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Orbital radius | 1 221 870 km = **21.0 Saturn radii** |
| Orbital speed | 5.572 km/s |
| Period | **15.945 d** |
| **Approach-side half-orbit** | **7.973 d** |
| Raise from 60 000 km park to Titan | 6.276 + 3.231 = 9.507 km/s |
| Transfer time, park → Titan | 3.238 d |
| Titan escape from 1200 km | 2.181 km/s |

There is the seven days. Not an alignment of planets: **Titan's own half-period.** Titan goes around Saturn in 15.945 days, so the approach-facing half of that orbit is **7.97 days** — the window during which Titan is on the side of Saturn your transfer arrives from. Miss it and you wait sixteen days for Titan to come back around. Seven days, once per Titan orbit, measured by the moon itself.

And then the thing that makes Titan the destination rather than Saturn. Titan's capture is cheap — shallow well, and a nitrogen atmosphere denser at the surface than Terra's. **Aerocapture is available at Titan and nowhere else on the route.** Not at Saturn: no surface, no defined entry corridor, and a ring plane in the way. Not at Jupiter: the radiation environment and the entry velocity are both lethal. Titan is the only body in the outer system where you can arrive and stop without spending propellant, and that — not composition, not the clouds — is why a crewed mission's twelfth moon of Saturn is Titan.

The Director's line about the clouds is, in this reading, the operational detail stated as scenery. *There is no seeing the surface from here. It's covered by a thick layer of cloud.* The thick layer of cloud is the brake.

What I will say is that the two numbers come from different axes, and that dissolves the inconsistency the film is usually accused of. **Seven days is kinetic** — Titan's half-period, a brake, a fuel budget, routine, measured. **Seventy years is not kinetic at all.** Under reaction propulsion a 70-year gate is unexplainable: the Terra–Saturn synodic period is 1.035 years, so a chemical or nuclear vehicle has a launch window to Saturn roughly *every year*. There is no reaction-propulsion reading of the film's seventy years. There is only a field-corridor reading, and that is the axis the NSFTDrive documents describe and the one this repo has not measured.

Which is the frame shift, and I think it is what the film is actually about. One week is a number you can check against a moon. Seventy years is a number handed to you by the people who measure your time, and the whole of *Gattaca* is about a man who was handed one of those at birth and declined to be its result.

There is a word for the first kind of number and I did not have it when I wrote that paragraph. **Wyrd**: *the* word, the one you get by charting the night sky or by simulating it. *The wyrd is the word of the stars in the sky. The wyrd is what happened and the wyrd is what will follow.* It has no tense, and that is arithmetic rather than mysticism — Keplerian elements with secular rates integrate in both directions out of the same expression, so one noun covers 2554 BC and 2554 AD. `Ephemeris.gd` has no past mode and no future mode. **Fate is wyrd with the instrument removed.** That is the inversion, and it is the whole plot. A wyrd is something you go and read: anyone with an aperture and patience can check it, and if your chart disagrees with the sky, the sky wins. A fate is handed to you — unreadable, unfalsifiable, issued by somebody else. **Vincent's genetic verdict is a counterfeit wyrd.** It borrows the grammar of the sky-word, *this is what happened and this is what will follow*, and strips out the only part that made it honest, which was that you could go and look.

So the seven days and the seventy years are not two readings of one number. Seven days is a wyrd — Titan's half-period, checkable against a moon, 7.973 days, computed above, wrong in a way you could catch me on. Seventy years is a fate: a figure with no instrument behind it, which is exactly why I had to file it OPEN in the paragraph above rather than dress it. The film states both in one breath from the same mouth, and the man listening spends the rest of the picture refusing to tell the difference by anything except going there.

## 9. The simulation

Everything above now has to run. The video that follows this page is `solar_system_explorer` flying it, and the flight must be true to the game — not a cutscene with the numbers painted on.

What the game already has, unchanged:

- **`NavModes`** — markers, playground, and `MODE_SIM_VIEW`, which the UI calls **"Real view"**
- **`RealismBudget`** — the Hohmann solver, the synodic-period inversion, the rocket equation, and the three Isp tiers used above
- **`PlotBoard`** — the course plot, with a window phase that genuinely waits the synodic interval. It already knows that some waits are too long to sit through: *"That's a long wait — skipping ahead on the calendar…"* It was written for the Saturn → Neptune case, which is the 35.858 years in §8.
- **`AstrogatorPanel`** — the read-only Δv ledger, shown in `PACE_ASTROGATOR` ("Rocket Science") and hidden in `PACE_KID` ("Quick Course"). Rocket Science is the mode this flight is flown in.

What has to be added: **Titan does not exist in the game.** Saturn appears in ten scripts; Titan appears in none. There is no satellite destination anywhere in `SolarData` — the flight model is heliocentric body-to-body. So the simulation needs a Saturn-centric arrival phase with Titan at 21.0 Saturn radii on a 15.945-day period, the half-period window as a real gate rather than a caption, and the aerocapture branch as a selectable arrival that the Astrogator ledger prices at zero propellant.

And the flight model needs to learn a route that is not one hop. A ladder is a chain where the intermediate worlds are flown past rather than parked at, which means each leg has to be solved from the previous leg's arrival point *at the clock it actually arrives* — so that the ship reaches Mars when Mars is really there. Stitching the legs and then re-timing them under one engine profile would be easier and would slide the ship off its own rungs, leaving slingshot markers that point at empty space. A chart may be ugly and it may be wrong about the future, but it may not lie about where the planets were.

That is the work. The ladder goes on the chart as three legs and two flyby markers, the Astrogator shows 19.082 km/s propulsive against 15.351 km/s free at Jupiter, the window phase waits 7.97 days for Titan instead of years for a planet, and the narration says what the burn is while the burn happens.

Two rungs, not three, and the correction is worth stating because it came out of building it. Saturn is on the route, but it is not a *rung*: we capture there, we do not swing past. A flyby and an arrival are opposite manoeuvres — one spends nothing and keeps the speed, the other spends 7.997 km/s to throw the speed away — and the §6 table lists Saturn's 132.08° deflection as a ceiling it could offer, not a turn this mission takes. So the flown ladder is Terra → Mars → Jupiter → Saturn-and-Titan: three legs, two slingshots, one capture. The propulsive total is unchanged, because the legs are unchanged.
