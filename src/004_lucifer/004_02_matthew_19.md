## I. The Method

παραβολή means and is phonetically 'parable' through 'throwing alongside' — meaning 'juxtaposition' or 'comparison'. It is a form of rhetoric.

δὲ means 'on the other hand' exclusively through 'but' when argumentative, while it means 'it follows' through 'and' when narrative. "What happened."

Narrative tells events in sequence with causal/temporal succession.

Argumentative is atemporal; abstract premise δὲ conclusion. "What follows."

Arguments may encapsulate a narrative, and narratives may encapsulate an argument.

In parable, the narrative form is itself inherently argumentative; the argument is expressed in and through the narrative structure rather than alongside it.

λέγω is 'to say' (assert, argument), 'to speak' (recount, narrative), or 'to tell' (direct, parable) through 'to count' or 'to enumerate' then through 'to gather' which itself is through 'to lay together'.

Arguments assert gathered boundaries and constraints proclaiming the conclusion that follows. Narratives tell gathered details. Parables create narrative for the listener to derive boundaries and constraints.

λέκτρον means 'bed', while in plural form it means 'marriage bed'. A marriage bed is assumed to produce a child; it follows.

## II. Homeric Etymology of λέγω

Homer uses λέγω exactly twice. In both cases the subjects lay down with someone else physically which forms a union, but there is nuance.

Both required speech. One a request, the other deception.

Helen is the mortal daughter of Zeus. She speaks to Paris directing him to put her to bed, δὲ asserting that he is beloved by Zeus, δὲ arguing in encapsulated narrative that sleep will be pleasurable.

The daughter of Zeus commands that his beloved Paris put her to bed so that they together may take pleasure in sweet sleep, presumably in Odysseus's physical marriage bed.

The holy union of the body produces a child.

> λέξον νῦν με τάχιστα διοτρεφές, ὄφρα καὶ ἤδη ὕπνῳ ὕπο γλυκερῷ ταρπώμεθα κοιμηθέντες
>
> *"Put me to bed now, beloved of Zeus, so that we may lie down and take pleasure from sweet sleep"*
>
> Il. 24.635 — Helen to Paris

The other scene is more complicated. Hera lures Zeus to a bed of flowers on Mount Ida. She hopes the sex will lull him to sleep, but his vigilance prevails forcing intervention from Hypnos, who puts him in a coma. The act of laying down lowered his vigilance enough for Hypnos to control his mind.

Hypnos invokes the same word, but the union in reference is not Zeus and Hera on the bed of flowers. It is his unholy union — the invasion of Zeus's mind. The result: Achilles, son of a god, dies.

The unholy union of the mind produces the death of a child.

> ἤτοι ἐγὼ μὲν ἔλεξα Διὸς νόον αἰγιόχοιο νήδυμος ἀμφιχυθείς
>
> *"For I, yes, I lulled the mind of Zeus of the goat-skin, having wrapped him round with deep sleep"*
>
> Il. 14.252 — Sleep speaking to Hera

The terminus in the etymological chain of λέγω is 'to gather together' or 'to form a union'.

Etymologists of λέκτρον who dismiss this as heathen bed are reductive. Their reduction erodes a literal narrative in The Iliad.

In ancient Greek grammar, the "movable consonant"—most famously movable nu (ν ἐφελκυστικόν, nu ephelkystikon, "nu drawn onto the end")—is a terminal consonant that appears or disappears at the end of specific verb and noun endings depending on the sound or punctuation that follows it.

1. How Movable Nu (-ν) Works

Movable -ν attaches strictly to words ending in -ε or -ι in four specific grammatical slots:
1. 3rd-person singular verbs ending in -ε: e.g., εἶπε vs. εἶπεν ("he/she said"), ἔλυσε vs. ἔλυσεν ("he/she loosed").
2. 3rd-person singular/plural verbs ending in -σι: e.g., ἐστί vs. ἐστίν ("is"), λύουσι vs. λύουσιν ("they loose"), λέγουσι vs. λέγουσιν ("they say").
3. Dative plural nouns/pronouns/participles ending in -σι: e.g., Νηρείδεσσι vs. Νηρείδεσσιν (as in Pindar Isthmian 6.6: Νηρείδεσσί τε), πᾶσι vs. πᾶσιν ("to all"), λόγοις / φύλαξι vs. φύλαξιν.
4. The numeral εἴκοσι vs. εἴκοσιν: ("twenty").

2. Why It Appears or Drops (Phonetic & Metrical Rules)

• Before a Vowel (Prevents Hiatus): When the next word begins with a vowel, movable -ν is inserted to keep two open vowels from colliding—e.g., εἶπεν αὐτῷ (eîpen autō, v. 17 & v. 18), where εἶπεν keeps its -ν because αὐτῷ starts with α-.
• At a Pause / Punctuation: Before a comma, period, or colon, movable -ν is routinely retained to close the syllable cleanly—e.g., εἷς ἐστιν ὁ ἀγαθός (before rough breathing ὁ, treated metrically or scribal convention) or at verse-end.
• Before a Consonant: In strict Attic prose, movable -ν drops before a following consonant (e.g., εἶπε δέ), except in poetry (Homer, Pindar, tragedy) and Koine Greek, where scribes and poets freely insert or omit -ν to lengthen a short syllable ("make position") for meter or rhythm.

3. Other "Movable" Terminal Consonants in Greek

Besides movable -ν, Greek has two minor movable consonants that operate on the exact same anti-hiatus principle:
• Movable Sigma (-ς): Appears on οὕτως ("thus/so") before a vowel, dropping to οὕτω before a consonant (and ἄχρις / μέχρις vs. ἄχρι / μέχρι).
• Movable Kappa (-κ / -χ): Appears on the negative particle οὐ ("not"):
	• οὐ before a consonant (e.g., Οὐ φονεύσεις, Οὐ μοιχεύσεις, Οὐ κλέψεις, Οὐ ψευδομαρτυρήσεις—all four verbs start with consonants φ, μ, κ, ψ, so Οὐ stays bare!).
	• οὐκ before a smooth-breathing vowel (e.g., οὐκ ἔστιν).
	• οὐχ before a rough-breathing vowel (e.g., οὐχ ὁρᾷς).

4. Direct Examples Right Inside Your Verses (Matt 19:17–18)

In Greek grammar and phonetics, τ (tau) functions as the structural boundary, demonstrative pointer, and fixed consonant anchor—standing in direct contrast to the fluid, mobile -ν.

1. Definite Article & Demonstrative Anchor (τό, τήν, τοῦ, τάς)

In classical and Koine Greek, τ- is the demonstrative stem of the definite article across all oblique cases and the neuter nominative (τό, τοῦ, τῷ, τόν, τήν, τά, τῶν, τοῖς, ταῖς, τούς, τάς):
• Deictic "Pointing" Function: Historically (in Homer), forms beginning with τ- were demonstrative pronouns ("this / that"), physically pointing at and isolating a specific object, clause, or boundary.
• Nominalizer / Framing Bracket (Τὸ in v. 18): When placed in front of an entire clause (Τὸ Οὐ φονεύσεις...), Τό acts as an enclosing bracket—locking an open statement into a single, fixed object.
• Contrast with ὁ / ἡ / οἱ / αἱ: Notice that the masculine/feminine nominative articles (ὁ, ἡ, οἱ, αἱ) drop the τ- in favor of rough breathing (῾), whereas every governed/oblique case (τήν, τοῦ, τάς, τό) locks in the τ- stem.

2. Phonetic Stop & Metrical Boundary (Voiceless Dental Stop)

Phonetically, τ is a voiceless dental stop (tenuis):
• Complete Closure: Unlike ν (a resonant nasal that can prolong, bridge, or attach/detach freely), τ requires the tongue to completely stop airflow against the teeth—acting as a hard phonetic wall/stop.
• Vowel-Collapse Trigger (Elision): Because τ is a hard consonant boundary, short vowels immediately before it routinely elide (collapse)—e.g., τὰ → τ᾽, τό → τ᾽—showing τ acting as a constraint rather than adding freedom.

3. Morphological & Numeral Functions of τ

• 3rd-Person Verb Marker (-ται, -το, -τω): Across Greek verb conjugation, -τ- marks the 3rd-person singular/plural boundary (the external "it/he/she/they" referent, e.g., γέγραπται, ἐγένετο) and the 3rd-person imperative (-τω, "let it be").
• Deictic Demonstrative Suffix (-το / οὗτος / τοῦτο): Attaches to pronouns (οὗτος / τοῦτο, "this right here") to pin down a specific referent in space or discourse.
• Numeral Value (τʹ = 300 / ,τ = 300,000): In Greek alphabetic numerals, τʹ represents the fixed integer 300 (and in ancient Greek acrophonic numerals, Τ stood for τάλαντον, a fixed standard weight/talent).
