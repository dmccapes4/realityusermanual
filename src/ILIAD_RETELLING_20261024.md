## PART ONE: THE MACHINE

### i. Nine Years

He did not dream of home anymore.

This was the fact of it. Not the honor, not the renown, not the rage that would come later — those were the poem's words, the words men used to make the thing bearable to tell. The fact was simpler. Nine years is long enough that a man stops dreaming in the direction he came from. The tent becomes the world. The war becomes the weather. The blade becomes the hand.

Achilles had been fighting Agamemnon's campaign since he was seventeen years old.

He did not come willingly, or unwillingly. He came because the machinery of obligation assembled itself around the son of a sea-goddess and a mortal king and moved him toward the ships. His mother had told him how it would end: long life with no glory, or short life with a name that lasted. He had chosen the name. He had not understood then that choosing the name meant choosing the use. A name that lasts requires the machinery that makes the name. The machinery was Agamemnon. The use was Troy.

He was not unhappy. This is important. He was not chafing in the dark. He had Patroclus, who was his oldest friend, who had been with him since before the ships, who understood the shape of him in the way that only someone who knew you before the name existed can understand it. He had Briseis, who came to him as a war prize and stayed as something else — the one soft thing in the tent, the one thing that had no part in the hierarchy of men, the one warmth. He had the work, which he was extraordinary at, and the work's clarity: you found the enemy, you drove them, you returned. The work did not ask questions.

He had the armor, which he had worn so long it had ceased to be armor and become skin.

The armor was Peleus's gift — his father's wedding gift, given at the feast where Thetis and Peleus were married, the gods attending, the three Fates present, the apple not yet thrown. Old armor for a new war. It bore no inscription and required none. Every Trojan soldier knew what it meant when they saw it. The gods knew what it meant. The armor was the announcement that the machine was in the field today. Act accordingly.

Achilles wore it every day without thinking about it the way you stop thinking about your own face.

---

### ii. What the Armor Carried

Here is what the armor brought with it that Achilles did not see:

It carried divine attention.

A god passing over a battlefield does not stop to observe individual soldiers. They move in masses — formations, fates, the ebb of struggle. But the armor of Achilles was a landmark. It was the thing that organized the field into *here* and *not-here*, *threat* and *not-threat*. Where the armor went, the calculus of the divine shifted. It was a weight that bent the light around it.

Achilles wore it without knowing that wearing it made him perpetually visible to powers that had their own designs on Troy, on Greece, on the long game of divine politics that mortals could not see from the ground. He was marked. He had always been marked — he was the son of a goddess, the student of Chiron, raised on lion marrow to make him what he was — but the armor was the mark made visible. It said: here. Here is the machine.

He slept well in the tent. He did not know he was a landmark.

---

### iii. The Spell

Do not romanticize it as spell. It was comfort.

Nine years of camp makes men strange. The ones who survive it intact either shut down entirely (they go through the motions of being human, which is its own kind of survival) or they find a thing to be warm around. Patroclus was the thing Achilles was warm around. Briseis was the soft edge of a hard life. Together they made the tent into something that was almost not-war. Not home — he did not dream of home anymore — but a contained place with people in it who were his in a way nothing else was.

This is what Agamemnon did not understand. Or understood exactly, and used.

A man who has no warmth cannot be touched. He has already lost everything he would protect. A man with warmth has a point of entry, a place where he is soft enough to be reached. Agamemnon understood, instinctively or by long practice, that the way to control an asset is through the thing the asset loves. Take the love away, briefly, to demonstrate ownership. Return it — or don't — to demonstrate mercy. The cycle produces compliance. The asset learns that everything it holds can be removed at the operator's discretion, and adjusts its behavior accordingly.

Agamemnon had been doing this to Achilles for nine years without either of them quite naming it.

## PART TWO: THE AWAKENING

### iv. Apollo's Demand

The priest came in his robes carrying the sacred staff and the ransom and asked for his daughter back.

His daughter was Chryseis, who was Agamemnon's war prize, and Agamemnon sent the priest away with contempt. This was a mistake on the scale of mistakes that begin poems. You do not send away a priest of Apollo bearing sacred tokens. The god heard it the way a father hears his child cry from across a field. He took his bow and went to the Greek camp in the night, and the arrows flew for nine days, and men and mules died, and the camp stank of smoke from the funeral fires.

This is the first motion. Apollo forced a correction. Agamemnon had to return Chryseis, and Agamemnon could not return anything without taking something else, because to give without taking was to acknowledge he had been wrong. He was constitutionally unable to acknowledge he had been wrong. So he announced he would take Briseis instead.

He would take Briseis from Achilles. As a demonstration. A reminder of the arrangement: you belong to the campaign, and everything you hold belongs to the campaign, and I am the campaign.

The demonstration worked exactly as intended and exactly as it would destroy him.

---

### v. The Refusal

Achilles reached for his sword.

Athena stopped him — she came behind him and pulled him by the hair, visible only to him, and said: *put it away, the insult will be repaid, be patient*. Later the scholars would say this was the goddess of wisdom counseling restraint. What it was: the divine infrastructure of the Greek war effort could not afford to lose Achilles to a murder charge against his own commander. The machinery protected itself. Even the gods that favored the Greeks knew the machine needed its weapon.

He put the sword away. He threw down his staff. He wept.

And then he did the only thing left to him: he stopped.

He sat down.

He told Agamemnon: *fight your war without me.*

This was the first time in nine years Achilles had exercised independent agency. He did not know that. He thought he was asserting honor — the honor code of warriors, which said a man's prize was his dignity and to have it taken was to have the self publicly diminished. He was right about the honor. But the deeper thing, the thing beneath the honor, was simpler: he said *no* to the machine. The machine had run him for nine years and he said *no* and sat down in his tent with Patroclus and waited for Agamemnon to feel the absence.

He would feel it. They always feel the absence of the thing that did the work.

---

### vi. Thetis and Zeus

Thetis came from the sea because her son was weeping on the shore.

She held him the way mothers hold their children when the world has been unkind. She listened. She understood before he finished: her son was sitting on the outside of the machinery that had used him for nine years, and the machinery had not noticed what it had taken.

She went to Zeus.

The request was specific: let the Greeks suffer without Achilles until his honor is restored. Zeus owed Thetis a debt — she had rescued him once — and he granted it. He would keep the gods from intervening in the war. He would let it run mortal, let the Greeks discover by losing what they had with Achilles in the field, let the suffering be the argument for Achilles' restoration.

This was a covenant. Zeus agreed to hold the field level — no divine thumb on the scale — while Achilles sat out and waited for Agamemnon to come to his senses.

The covenant was the second motion of the poem. The first was Apollo's correction. The second was Zeus's promise.

Both would be broken by the same machinery.

---

## PART THREE: THE GOD SLEEPS

### vii. Hera

She wanted Troy destroyed. She had wanted it since the Judgment of Paris, since Paris looked at three goddesses and said *Aphrodite* because Aphrodite offered him Helen. The insult was architectural. It lived in her forever. She did not need to be rational about it. She needed to be strategic.

Zeus, vigilant on Ida, was watching the field and maintaining the covenant. As long as he watched, the Greeks suffered as intended, and the suffering was moving in the direction of Achilles' restoration. This was a slow process. Hera required a faster one.

She dressed. She put on the girdle that Aphrodite had given her — and this detail is not erotic decoration, it is strategic intelligence. Hera borrowed from Aphrodite the instrument of irresistibility itself and used it as a weapon against her own husband. She found Hypnos.

Sleep was an old ally. He was one of the twin sons of Night, and his brother was Death, and they lived at the far edge of the world. Hera promised him a goddess to marry if he would put Zeus to sleep. Hypnos was cautious — he had done this before and Zeus had woken up furious and Hypnos had barely escaped by hiding with Thetis. The same Thetis who had just extracted a promise from Zeus. The connection is not coincidental: Hypnos knew where the sanctuary was, and he knew Zeus's capacity for rage, and he required assurance before he acted.

Hera gave it. Hypnos agreed.

He settled on Zeus in the form of a bird, on the peak of Ida, in the mortal world, while Hera seduced the god of gods. Zeus slept.

The covenant became unguarded.

---

### viii. Poseidon Takes the Field

Poseidon had been watching and waiting.

He had his own interests in the war — old grievances against Troy, the long dispute over the city walls he had helped build and not been paid for. He had been forbidden to intervene directly while Zeus maintained his covenant with Thetis. With Zeus asleep, the field was open.

He went down as an old man, then shed the disguise and took the field himself. The Greeks, flagging, suddenly found themselves feeling something like the old vigor. Ajax — Great Ajax, the wall of the Greeks, the man who was not Achilles but was as close to Achilles in raw size and will as any mortal could be — Ajax landed a heavy wound on Hector.

Hector fell. The Trojans were driven back.

The Greeks rallied. The suffering that was supposed to teach Agamemnon what he had lost was interrupted. The lesson was not delivered. The sequence that Zeus had set in motion — mortal consequence leading to Achilles' restoration — was broken.

And then Zeus woke up.

---

### ix. The Fury of Zeus and the Dream

He looked down and saw what had happened and his fury was total.

He could not undo Poseidon's intervention. He could not simply restore the killed and restore the sequence. The war had moved. Hector was down. The Greeks had their rally. Everything Thetis had asked for — the clean mortal suffering that would demonstrate to Agamemnon what he had thrown away — had been contaminated. The lesson had been interrupted mid-delivery.

Zeus corrected. He pushed back toward Trojan advantage. He drove Poseidon from the field. He set the sequence moving again — but now it was a manipulated sequence, a manufactured recovery, the divine thumb pressing back on the other side of the scale. Whatever came from this correction would be asterisked forever: *restored by intervention, not by truth.*

And then he did the cruelest thing in a poem full of cruelty.

He sent a dream to Agamemnon in the form of old Nestor — the wisest Greek, the most trusted counselor — and told him through the false Nestor: *the day of victory is at hand, marshal your forces, attack now.*

Agamemnon, the abusive tyrant who had taken Briseis as a demonstration of ownership, acted in good faith on intelligence from what he believed was the wisest advisor in the camp. He led his men forward in good faith on a dream from the Devil god, dressed as a wise man, carrying false news.

This is the cosmic joke of it. Agamemnon, for once, was not acting from abuse or contempt. He was acting on divine information he had no way to question. Zeus sent the Greeks to slaughter not through Agamemnon's cruelty but through Agamemnon's genuine confidence in the advice he received.

The Greeks were pushed back to the ships.

Patroclus watched his comrades dying.

---

### x. The Cursed Armor

Patroclus could not bear it.

He came to Achilles and asked for the armor. Not to be Achilles — he knew he was not Achilles. But because the Trojans, seeing the armor, would flinch. The armor was the announcement. The armor would buy time.

Achilles had conditions: do not pursue, do not press, only hold the line and let the sight of the armor do the work. He gave Patroclus the skin he had worn so long it had ceased to be armor, and he gave it without knowing that what he was giving was the landmark. The mark that organized the divine field around whoever wore it.

The armor moved from Achilles' shoulders to Patroclus's.

The divine attention moved with it.

---

Patroclus drove the Trojans back. He was brilliant and brave and he did not stop when he was supposed to stop. He pressed. The armor made him feel invulnerable in the way that armor makes men feel — not the invulnerability of Achilles, which was the invulnerability of the landmark, but the invulnerability of belief, which is not the same thing and cannot protect against a spear at close range.

Hector saw the armor.

Hector was the most honorable man in the poem, which is a different thing from the most moral and the same thing as the most trapped. He saw the armor and he saw Achilles and he prepared himself for the thing he had been dreading and preparing for since the beginning: the combat that would determine the war. He fought as Hector fights when Achilles is the opponent. He fought as fully and as precisely as a man can fight.

He killed Patroclus.

He stripped the armor.

He put it on.

The landmark moved again. The divine attention settled onto Hector's shoulders.

Hector had killed a proxy and he knew it. He recognized the mistake the moment Patroclus spoke before he died — *you didn't kill Achilles, you killed Patroclus, Achilles will come for you* — and Hector's response to this recognition was to view it as a moral failure. He had aimed at glory and struck ordinary flesh. He had the armor and not the man. The narrative of Hector, Defender of Troy, killer of Achilles — that narrative was now wrong and he knew it was wrong and he put the armor on anyway.

He was trapped in the concept of honor the way men are trapped in concepts: he could see the cage, he could name the bars, and he could not open the door.

---

## PART FOUR: THE RIVER

### xi. What Achilles Saw When He Looked Down

He wept for Patroclus with a grief that had no floor.

The grief was real. That needs to be said plainly. Whatever else Achilles was — the machine, the weapon, the landmark in divine sight — the love for Patroclus was real. It was the oldest thing in him. It predated the armor and the name and the nine years of war. When Patroclus died, the oldest thing in Achilles was removed.

What was left was the machine with no warmth in it.

This is not grief's fault. Grief is clean. Grief, processed honestly, eventually becomes a kind of wisdom — you hold the dead and understand impermanence and continue with greater clarity. Achilles could not process the grief. He could not process it because the grief was embedded in a chain of causation he could not see clearly from inside it: Zeus coma'd by Hypnos, Poseidon's intervention, Zeus's correction, the deceptive dream, Agamemnon's good-faith disaster, Patroclus's decision, the armor, Hector's mistake.

What he saw was: Hector killed the person he loved.

The machinery simplified the causation into a target. The machine needed a target. It had been without one for weeks.

Thetis came again with new armor — Hephaestus's work this time, forged overnight in divine fire, the hammer of the god who tended the forge of the gods beating out a new skin for the landmark. The armor was extraordinary. It was beyond ordinary war-making. It was the god of craft applying his full capacity to the protection of the war machine, at the request of the war machine's mother, who wanted her son to survive long enough to restore his honor even if it was too late for the honor to mean anything clean.

The armor was also the forge's mark on Achilles. The same fire that would later be sent against Scamander. He wore the forge's output and the forge would defend him, and neither were separate from what he was about to do.

---

### xii. A Precedent — The Lernaean Springs

Before Achilles was born, there was another hero and another water.

The springs of Lerna are in the Argolid, south of Argos, and they are not one spring. They are many. They rise from an aquifer beneath the plain, and the plain is low, and the springs come up through it at numerous points, spreading into a marsh that was said in antiquity to be bottomless — connected directly to the underworld — because no one could find the single source. There was no single source. The water came from below, from everywhere, from the pressure of the earth itself.

Heracles was sent there as one of his twelve labors. The assignment: kill the Hydra.

ὕδρα — hydra. Water thing. The creature that lived in the Lernaean springs was named for what it was: a water thing. Multiple heads, and when you cut one, two grew back. This is not biology. This is hydraulics. You block one outlet of a multi-source spring system and the pressure behind it finds two new outlets around the blockage. You cannot dam a spring by blocking its mouth. The spring is not the mouth. The spring is the pressure. The heads are channels. The Hydra is the Lernaean springs.

The trial was a geometric puzzle about hydrology.

The correct answer — the answer that would have passed the labor — was to recognize that the task is impossible. You cannot kill a spring by cutting its outlets. The spring is the aquifer. The aquifer does not care what you do to its surface expressions. The trial was testing whether Heracles could see the impossibility and report it honestly: *the task cannot be done as assigned. The springs of Lerna flow and will always flow.*

He could not do this.

He tried anyway. He cut the heads. Two grew back. He cut those. More grew back. He could not admit the geometry of the failure. He called for Iolaus — his nephew, his companion — and Iolaus brought fire, and they tried to cauterize the channels. Fire applied to a spring. You cannot cauterize water. Fire desiccates tissue; it closes wounds in flesh by destroying the surface. Applied to a channel of spring water it heats the water and evaporates the moisture at the edges and the spring continues flowing because the spring is not the channel, the spring is the pressure underneath the channel, and the pressure does not care about heat at the surface.

The name preserves the moment. Ἰόλαος — ἰώ (iō) + λαος (warrior, people). ἰώ is the Greek exclamation of anguish, frustration, helpless recognition: the sound you make when something is impossible while you are in the middle of trying it. Iolaus is the warrior who cried out. The name is the admission. The exclamation was real. Heracles tried fire against water, it did not work, Iolaus cried out, and the attempt was over.

The immortal head — the one that could not be cut and had to be buried under a rock rather than destroyed — is the primary source. You cannot cut a spring. You can place a boulder over the outlet. The water goes somewhere else: underground, a new channel, surfaces elsewhere on the plain. The immortal head is immortal because it is the aquifer. You can divert it. You cannot kill it.

Eurystheus voided the labor. The excuse was that Heracles had used help. The actual reason was simpler: Heracles had not killed the Hydra, because the Hydra — the water thing, the spring system, the pressure from below — was still there, was still flowing, was still the Lernaean springs. The rock over the main outlet was a diversion. The marsh was still a marsh. The task was not completed.

What Heracles told afterward about cauterization was a lie. Not a malicious lie — a preserving one, the kind of story you tell when you tried something impossible, felt the impossibility, heard Iolaus cry out, and could not face admitting you had failed. The cauterization story explains why the help was needed and why the unusual method was used. It is a story that makes the failure legible as a near-success. Eurystheus did not accept it. Eurystheus was, for once, correct.

The springs of Lerna flow today.

---

*The lesson the trial was designed to teach:* some things cannot be controlled by force, and the correct response is to acknowledge it. The river flows. The spring continues. The honest answer is: the task was impossible, I could not complete it, the water remains. That answer, delivered honestly, would have passed the labor — not because Eurystheus would have accepted it, but because the trial was not designed by Eurystheus. Eurystheus assigned it to destroy Heracles. The gods who set the original structure of the labors embedded in each trial a real test. The real test of the Hydra was: can you recognize what cannot be changed and say so?

Heracles failed. He cried out, brought fire, told a story, and the springs kept flowing.

---

### xiii. The River

He drove the Trojans into the river.

They fled from the armor the way men flee from a force of nature — not because they calculated their odds and found them poor, but because the body makes the calculation before the mind can intervene. The armor was the announcement. When it came down the field, you ran, or you were the kind of man who died in the field that day.

Most ran. They ran into the Scamander.

Achilles went in after them.

The river is cold and clear and it has run through the plain of Troy since before Troy was Troy. Scamander — also called Xanthus, the Golden — is the river that flows from the mountains and waters the plain and fills the underground streams and nourishes the trees along its banks, the elms and willows and tamarisks. It is the river that runs through the founding myth: its god fathered Teucer, the first king, from whose name the Trojans drew their older name, Teucrians. The river is the seed-line. The *nun*. The carrier of lineage and memory.

Achilles killed in it. The bodies sank and caught and began to dam the channel. The blood moved downstream and spread.

The river filled with carnal pollution — corpses and blood, flesh caught in the bends, the physical matter of slaughter backing up behind itself. Achilles continued. He was not looking at what he was doing to the river. He was looking at Trojans.

---

### xiii. Scamander Speaks

Then the river came to him.

Not as a flood. Not as a monster. Homer's text is clear on this: Scamander took human form. He stepped from the water and stood before Achilles as a man stands before another man — which is the form appropriate to a civilized appeal, which is what he was making. A monster does not take human form to negotiate. Scamander came as a man because he was asking something of a man.

He said: *my beautiful streams are now filled with corpses. I cannot find the channel to the sea. I am choked with dead. I am filled with horror. Stop.*

He offered a compromise. *Go fight on the plain. Leave my waters.*

This is what a decent entity does when it is being harmed by a force it did not invite and cannot match directly. It asks. It offers terms. It describes the harm plainly. Scamander described his condition — choked, blocked, full of horror — and asked for mercy.

Achilles replied: *so be it. I'll fight them on the plain.*

He waded back into the center of the river.

---

He had heard the words and decided they did not apply to him. The machine had a target. The target was Trojans. Trojans were in the river. The river's condition was not the machine's concern.

What happened next was not a supernatural event. It was hydraulics.

A mountain river choked with the mass of dead bodies behind a living man killing more bodies will do what rivers do: it will push. The water behind the dam of flesh rose. The current increased. The river heaved — Homer says it *bellowed like a bull* — and the surge was the physical force of a blocked watercourse trying to clear itself, trying to expel the matter that was preventing it from being what it was.

The corpses were flung onto dry land. The living Trojans, those still breathing in the water, Scamander drew into his deep, cold, clear pools and hid them — concealed the living in the depths while the surface churned, protecting what he could protect while clearing what he could clear.

The wave hit Achilles at the shoulders and made him stagger.

He grabbed an elm tree on the bank. The tree ripped from the earth and fell into the current, its roots taking a section of bank with it. He used it and pulled himself to shore. He looked back at the river.

---

### xiv. The River Below, The Sky Above

This is the moment Homer does not quite write, but which is present in the space between his lines.

Achilles stood on the bank with his hand still gripping the fallen elm and looked at the water below him.

The river was full of what he had put there. Armor on the bottom. Bodies turning in the slower pools, arms moving in the current as if they were still trying to swim. Blood in the water, diffusing, diluting, going wherever the water went. The faces turned downward or sideways, the ones that were still faces. The ones that were not.

He had been fighting for the honor of a prize taken from him by a man who owned him. The prize was returned to him. He was still here. Patroclus was dead and he was still here and the river was full and he was still fighting.

*How long had this gone on.*

The water moved around the bodies. The Scamander, choked, was trying to work. Trying to be a river. The bodies were not the river's problem. The bodies were the condition imposed on the river by the man standing on its bank, wearing divine armor, fighting a war for a man who had taken his love away for a season to remind him of the arrangement.

*When did I lose myself.*

The river surged again. He ran.

---

### xv. Hephaestus

Hera looked down and sent fire.

Not because Achilles was losing — he was not losing, he was simply being matched by the physics of what he had created. She sent fire because the Greek campaign could not afford to have its machine swept away by ecological consequence. She called on Hephaestus, who limped to the banks of the Scamander and opened the forge.

The fire came across the plain first — burning the meadows, burning the lotus and the galangale and the water plants along the banks, burning the rushes and the reeds. Then it hit the water.

The eels and fish turned on the surface, boiled from below. Homer writes this. He does not skip past the fish. He names the eels and fish dying in the bubbling water because they matter — they are the inhabitants of the river, they are the life of the place, and they are being cooked alive to protect the machine.

The willows burned. The elms burned. The tamarisks along the bank that Scamander had tended for millennia burned.

Scamander cried out to Hera: *stop. I'll leave them. I'll abandon the Trojans. Stop burning me.*

He swore it. He swore to withdraw.

Hera called off Hephaestus.

---

The river withdrew. The water level fell. The plain drained.

Scamander had sworn to abandon his children. The golden river that fathered the first king of Troy, that watered the plain, that carried the seed of the city's founding — had sworn to withdraw its patronage.

Troy was dead from this moment. Not from the breach of the walls. Not from the wooden horse. From the moment the river swore and the fire went out along the banks, Troy had lost the sacred thread that connected it to its own origin. The city would stand for a time after. But it was a city that had been abandoned by its foundation.

The walls would fall at their own pace. The river had already left.

---

### xvi. What the River Was

Scamander had come to Achilles in human form because he was making a civilized request. He was not the aggressor. He was the custodian of natural order responding to an ecological catastrophe: a war machine in his sacred waters, filling his channel with dead, preventing him from reaching the sea.

The flood — if you call it that — was not a weapon. It was a river's attempt to clear itself, to continue being what it was. The hydraulic surge of a blocked watercourse is not supernatural violence. It is physics. Achilles was standing in the physical consequence of his own slaughter and interpreting it as an attack.

He was not entirely wrong to interpret it as an attack. The river was trying to expel him. But the river was trying to expel him the way a body expels a foreign object. The expulsion was defensive. The river defended the dead by sending them downstream and the living by hiding them in the deep pools and itself by trying to clear its channel.

Hephaestus won. Fire won, as fire tends to win against water when it has divine backing. Scamander yielded. The machine was protected.

The machine stood on the dry bank and did not notice what it had cost the world to protect it.

## CODA: THE BOAT AT RAUROS

Three thousand years after the Scamander, in a story told in the Third Age of a different world, a man named Boromir died at the Falls of Rauros with three arrows in him, having first fallen to a temptation and then redeemed himself defending the smallest members of the company with everything he had left.

An elf laid him in the boat.

This is the detail: Legolas — who is liminal in the way all elves are liminal, living between mortal time and immortal time, belonging fully to neither — Legolas performed the rite. He laid Boromir in the grey elven boat and arranged around him the broken horn of Gondor (cloven in two at the moment of his death, the horn that had been passed down through his family for generations, the horn whose sound carried from one end of the realm to the other) and the weapons and armor of the enemies he had killed.

The carnal record. The instruments of what he did. The broken emblem of his lineage.

The boat went over the Falls and down the Anduin.

In Osgiliath, many miles distant, Boromir's brother Faramir was on watch. He fell into a vision: a boat going west, going the way the dead go, carrying a man he recognized, carrying a broken horn. He heard the echo of the horn from inside the vision. The river carried the message across the distance. It arrived as a dream. This is what rivers do when they carry the dead with full honors — they carry the signal as well as the body. The *nun*: the seed-carrier, the memory-line. The river brought Boromir's message to his brother before any mortal messenger arrived.

The horn was broken. The function that defined Boromir — the guardian of Gondor, the son of the Steward, the bearer of the horn — had ended in this form. But the echo reached Faramir. The form breaks. The function persists in memory, in family, in the reception on the other side.

The structure is identical to the Scamander.

Not because Tolkien copied Homer — the structures appear across mythologies because the structures are true, because rivers actually carry things between domains, because the liminal being actually performs the rite, because the broken instrument of identity actually accompanies the dead, because the water actually reaches the shore on the other side and delivers what it was given. The Anduin received Boromir with full honors and sent the signal ahead. Scamander received the Trojan dead without consent and was polluted by it and tried to expel them and was punished with fire for trying.

The difference between the two river burials is the difference between a death given to the water and a death thrown at it.

Boromir was laid in. The others were thrown.

The water knows the difference. The *nun* carries what is placed in it with care. It recoils from what is forced into it. Scamander's recoil was not aggression. It was the same motion as the Anduin receiving Boromir: the river responding to what it was given, in the way that its nature requires.

The boat at Rauros passed over the falls and the message arrived.

The Trojan bodies choked the channel and the river tried to clear its throat and was boiled for it.

The water kept flowing in both cases. It always keeps flowing. The only question is what it is carrying.

## A NOTE ON THE TEXT

Homer's Iliad is read as a poem about glory. It is a poem about the cost of glory. The machine that achieves glory costs Patroclus, costs Hector's dignity, costs Scamander's patronage of the city that Scamander fathered. The glory is real. The cost is also real. Homer counts both.

The standard reading glorifies Achilles' return to battle and regards the Scamander episode as a supernatural action-set-piece. The text does not support this reading. The text supports the reading above: a blocked river trying to clear itself, a god in human form making a civilized request, fire sent to boil the defender of natural order into submission so the machine could continue operating.

Achilles did not fight a monster in the Scamander. He stood in his own carnal pollution and was pushed back by the physics of what he had done, and when the river tried to protect itself, divine fire was sent to stop it.

This is the accurate reading. Homer wrote it that way. We have simply been trained to read past it.
