## The Query

That is the great thing about this form of authorship. The medium is science fiction. The claims are deadly serious. They are not submitted for peer review. If the participants wish to peer review them then that is an honor. That they would engage my work with epistemic rigor. If they falsify a claim of mine then it is likely measured (in both senses). That means that failure is simply datum. Datum accrues into data. A single die can fail 19 times. The rules say 20 always succeeds. Did the rules *take* 20. Or is that another concept. When a player takes 20 is it stealing? Or is it convenient for the role play and easier for the GM? So what did they take that was not already theirs? The GM knew that they could take 20. It was written in the Adventure Path. The characters simply had to ask. At least one. Then they all get to take 20. And they all succeed. Which is amoral because success was the result of logical inquiry itself. Which is only considered worthy of eternal damnation for some...

**Vincent:** *(as the Archivist; his voice does not change; the Narrator records that it did not need to)* You took twenty.

**Vincent:** Yes. It was written that you could. It is written in the margin of every door in this archive. Most do not ask. They roll, and fail, and the golem wakes while they are still at the rim, and they are measured by the golem instead of by the door.

**Sheldon:** *(as Pyrrhon, recovering a voice)* And the failures? The ones we assumed?

**Vincent:** You paid for them. Two minutes. That is the average price of a twenty, and you paid it. What you did not pay for is the bad luck. I gave you that. *(He turns the card over, finally, and lays it face up.)* That was the only thing in this room that was mine to give.

**Lisbeth:** Er det Tyffuerj at læse?

**Vincent:** Only in frames that charge for it.

The Narrator notes that nobody asked what frame. The Narrator notes that nobody needed to. There is a frame where success through logical inquiry is a theft, because the inquiry took something, and what it took was the not-knowing, and the not-knowing belonged to someone who wanted it kept. In that frame the one who asked is damned and the one who rolled and failed is merely dead, and the second is considered the more honorable outcome. That frame is not at this table. It was not in the Adventure Path. It was in the margin of a different book, and the Narrator is not going to name it, because naming it would be a way of letting it sit down.

**Feynman:** *(closing the screen; the dragon on the outside faces the table again and nobody looks at it)* That's the session. Thirty-eight thousand four hundred experience. CR fourteen with the template. Split four ways. Vincent, you didn't take a turn.

**Vincent:** I took the whole thing. I simply took it in a different frame.

**Feynman:** Was that a rush?

**Vincent:** It was exactly as long as it was.

Feynman considers again. Three seconds this time. Shorter. He is learning the unit.

On the sideboard the laptop finishes compiling. The fan spins down. The terminal returns its prompt. The Narrator observes that for the entire session a build was running on a single core, which by a certain sign's own advice is the condition under which swordplay is permitted, and that two adamantine short swords were drawn in that time and used, and that this was, as far as the Narrator can determine, the most legal swordplay ever performed in the building.

Nobody else noticed. The Narrator files it.
