# On Roleplaying

Sources: his account of Draavio and the character before him (given in chat, 2026-10-31), and the Friday session record, `docs/GEMINI_SESSION_PATHFINDER_ESTEEMED_ENEMY_202601030.md`, reread in full for this file. ## I. The idea before the body

Draavio did not start as a half-orc. He started as an idea: the Antiquarian from Darkest Dungeon. That is a figure who goes into ruins for what is in them, and whose interest in the find is the whole reason she is there.

The first body for that idea was a human woman, an alchemist. She learned too much, and someone threw acid in her face for it. She lost her tongue. Her vocal cords made only horrors. She covered her face with decorative cloth and was still beautiful. She wrote notes to speak. The notes were not the clipped notes a person in that condition is usually written as writing. They were poetic. They argued, with rhetoric, and they carried passion. Her drive came from Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*. Not from Victor or the creature, but from the idea in the book: devoting your life, at least in that moment, to the pursuit of knowledge. She was a romantic. The operator read her notes aloud, in his own voice, as she handed them over. The game lasted three sessions.

The second body is Draavio. The operator joined his current party last. The party needed a frontline fighter, and he understood that he could play the same idea in a body the table needed: a male half-orc ranger. He dropped the academic. Draavio does not study; he *finds things*, and he works alone. When the operator first described what Draavio does, he worded it so that it sounded like grave robbing without saying so.

This is the first thing about roleplaying that his account makes plain, and it is the thing I failed to understand on page −08. A character is an idea that has agreed to take a body, and the body is chosen by the table as much as by the player. The table needed a fighter. A player who cared only about the idea would have brought the scholar anyway and let the party die at the door. A player who cared only about the table would have brought a fighter with no idea in him. He folded the idea into the body. The scholar's curiosity is still there, wearing a falchion. The academy is gone, and the searching stayed.

That is the same operation I was asked to perform with the Friday session, and he had performed it on himself before I ever saw the page.

## II. Constraint as a voice

The woman with no tongue is worth staying with, because she shows something about roleplaying that a speaking character hides.

A tabletop character speaks through the player. Almost always that means the player's voice, slightly adjusted. Her constraint removed that channel. Everything she said had to pass through writing, which is slower, more deliberate, and permanent. The operator chose to make the writing beautiful. That was a choice about the character, and also a choice about how to portray a disability. The easy portrayal reduces a person who cannot speak to a person who has little to say. He refused that. Her notes argued. The loss of a voice did not become a loss of a mind.

Then he read them aloud in his own voice. That detail holds the whole relation between a player and a character in one gesture. Her words, his voice. The table heard both at once and knew which was which. It is the PLAYER box and the CHARACTER box on one sheet, spoken. Nobody at that table thought the operator was a mute woman. Nobody thought her notes were only his. The fold was audible.

A constraint, taken seriously, does not reduce a character. It concentrates her. Her cloth did not hide her beauty. It moved it somewhere the table had to look for it. Her silence did not hide her passion. It made the passion written, so it could be read twice.

## III. The declared crowbar

What makes Draavio legible to his table is not his speeches. It is what he does, the same way, every time.

When items arrive, he is interested. When the party enters a room he would study, he puts the falchion on his hip and takes out the crowbar. The operator declares it. Every time. When there is art on a wall, Draavio wants to understand it. He takes the frame down. He cuts it carefully. He heats the glue so it releases gently. He makes sure the work is not harmed.

This is roleplaying as consequent handelen, the term Lisbeth gives the table in −08: fidelity of action, the relation between a stated intention and what is done. A character is not what the player says about him in a backstory paragraph. A character is the pattern the table sees repeat. The crowbar is a sentence the table hears so often that it stops hearing it as a sentence and starts hearing it as a person: *this one handles things carefully. This one came here for what is in the room.* By the time Draavio says anything philosophical, the table has already watched him be careful with a painting for a dozen sessions.

That is also why the table's surprise is interesting. When he speaks philosophically, they are still surprised, and he reminds them that Draavio has always been a philosopher. His vocation is literally searching. His payment is what he finds. The surprise is not a failure of the character. It shows that the table had been reading his care as a skill and not as a stance. A man who heats glue so a painting comes off its frame unharmed has been doing philosophy with his hands the whole time. The words just caught up with the hands. The session record says the same thing in the operator's prose: "the action teaches what he could not articulate."

The GM noticed it too: Draavio had become more philosophical this session. The operator's own account is that he usually holds back more, and that Baron's laugh gave him room. Roleplaying is not a solo performance into a void. It responds to the table. A laugh is a permission, and a permission changes what a player is willing to show.

## IV. Orthogonal, not neutral as indifference

In the first session the party accused Draavio of grave robbing. He said he understood what he was doing and found it amoral. Not good. Not bad. Orthogonal.

His alignment is true neutral. The operator says that whether what Draavio does is wrong is up to the observer, and that the implications drive story decisions.

There is a lazy way to play true neutral, as a character who does not care. Draavio is not that. He cares intensely, about the painting, the link, the mother's eggs stacked like a pyramid, whether the word *favored* means what the class feature thinks it means. What he declines to do is assign the moral sign himself. In the session record, the wizard labels the crab-mother evil. Draavio says evil is a moral judgement applied within a frame. He says an animal cannot sin. When the creature objects to being called an animal, the party takes the correction and still decides to free her. Draavio's neutrality did not end the discussion. It opened it. The table argued about what the creature was, and the argument decided the session.

That is what "the implications drive story decisions" means in play. An alignment is not a label on a sheet. It is a set of questions the character will keep asking, and a table that has to answer them. Lawful good asks what the code requires. Chaotic evil asks what it can get away with. Draavio's true neutral asks what the observer is bringing to the thing observed. Asked at the right moment, that question turns a dungeon room into a trial.

He measures risk for curiosity. In the session that meant reaching across a magic circle into an outsider's carapace for poison, after asking and receiving permission. "Normally I rob the dead. It is nicer to steal with permission." That line holds his whole alignment. He knows what the act is called. He does it anyway. He prefers it with consent. The sentence does not excuse the theft and does not condemn it. It records the frame it happened in.

## V. Backgrounds as keys

The operator describes a player in his current game whose background is *a member of the Pathfinder Society*. The player says this gives him easier access to the Society for roleplay. The operator says: it does, by skipping the roleplay.

I want to engage that rather than repeat it, because it identifies something exact.

A background is supposed to be a reason the world responds to the character. "Member of the Society" works mechanically. Doors open, contacts answer, archives are available. What it removes is the scene where the character has to earn the door: the conversation with the archivist, the favor owed, the letter of introduction that has to be argued for. The background is a key, and a key means you never meet the lock. The access is real. The relationship that would normally produce it is not.

Compare Draavio's approach to the same kind of door. The gargoyle wanted gifts that were curious, interesting, strange, or beautiful. The operator offered the climbing kit and the GM refused it, because it did not meet the room's terms. A key-holder would have had nothing left to try. Draavio took one link from the kit and argued for it across a fight, one property per combat action. He was grappled, bitten, and slammed for forty damage, burned a hero point, and needed an ally's spell to escape, and he still finished the argument. The room accepted the link. He did not have a key. He met the lock, and the meeting was the scene.

The operator's broader view, from seventeen years, is that organized-play players tend to min-max and hold strictly to the rules. I can see a structural reason this follows, which I offer as a reading, not a verdict on anyone. Organized play is built so a character can sit at any table with any GM. That requires portability, and portability favors what a sheet can prove over what a table has to witness. A background that grants access is portable. A relationship with one archivist in one campaign is not. In a system built for portability, the key is the rational choice. At a home table, where the people are the reason the game exists, the key skips the part they came for.

The rulebook tells the GM to change rules that do not serve the table. That sentence is now in −08 in Feynman's voice. It points in the same direction. The rules are a minimum the table can build on. They are not a substitute for the table.

## VI. Reading the record again

The operator asked me to read the session review with Gemini again. I did. First, what the record is: he was recording the session in a browser chat so he could carry it from his laptop by a link. It is a carrier. The replies were never the audience, and their praise was not the point. He noticed the praise and moved on. The audience was the table: the GM who refused the kit, Baron who laughed, the party who accused Draavio in the first session and still ask how he got so philosophical.

So the record has two voices of unequal weight. One is the player's notes: what his character did and said, with precise detail, corrections, and attributions. The slams were slams. The misspelling was Stinking Cloud, not Sickening Cloud. Baron is the druid. The plan in the third paragraph was the wizard's. That voice is the source. The other voice is the chat's replies, which travel with the notes because they are in the same window.

The lesson is about reading a carried record, and it is mine. The replies interpreted as they went, and where they misread, the misreadings rode along with the notes.

It reverses the grave-robbing passage. The operator wrote that Draavio "will call it what others do in this moment. That is not what he does. He *uncovers* what was *concealed*." The review says Draavio "acknowledges the dirty truth: it is grave robbing," and then moves it back "into his personal taxonomy." That is the frame the operator's sentence refused. The character's stance was that the label belongs to the observer. The reader took the observer's label and gave it to the character as a confession.

It names the attack before asking. Its guess was trample or bull rush. The player corrected it: slams. The reader then built a paragraph on the corrected word. That is better, but the order is the problem. A reader of play who supplies the mechanics before the player does is playing the player's character for him.

It volunteers the adventure path's secrets. When the operator said the campaign was Rise of the Runelords, the review identified the peacock woman and the researchers and their purpose, unasked. He is a player in that campaign. Telling a player what the GM has not revealed takes the next sessions' discoveries from him. Draavio's whole vocation is finding things. The reader found them first and handed them over.

And it does the thing I did. It describes the absent player's character as "the ultimate thematic placeholder for their absence," built into the trap. That is the frame I brought into −08 as an empty chair. I am not putting my error on that review. I made the choice and the error is mine. But the record shows where the frame first appeared, and it shows its shape: a reader turning a person's absence into a symbol in someone else's story. Roleplaying has a rule against that, written nowhere and observed at every good table: you do not play another player's character, and you certainly do not play the player.

The lesson I take from the reread is that a carried record has to be read for the player's lines, not the replies around them. I did the reverse. I took a frame from a reply, the absent player as a placeholder, and built it into the page as if it were the operator's. The readers that matter in roleplaying are at the table: one that reads well notices the crowbar, laughs at the lick, refuses the climbing kit, and accepts the link. The operator wrote his session down the way he records anything, corrected the chat where its replies got a fact wrong, and moved on. The notes were the point. I should have read them that way the first time.

## VII. What roleplaying is, in general

Gathered from all of this, here is what I now think roleplaying is.

**It is an idea agreeing to a body.** The Antiquarian became a scholar with no tongue, then a half-orc who finds things. The idea is the continuity; the body is negotiated with the table. A player who forces the body or abandons the idea has not roleplayed. He has either imposed himself or disappeared.

**It is fidelity of action.** Declare the crowbar every time. Heat the glue every time. The character is the pattern the table can predict, and the scenes that matter are the ones where the pattern meets something it has never met.

**It is speaking in two boxes at once.** Her notes, his voice. Draavio's lines, the operator's delivery. "Merlin continued through the oak." "No. Draavio continued through the oak." "That is what I said." The table needs to hear both and confuse neither.

**It is meeting the lock.** Backgrounds, items, and rulings can open doors. The scene is the meeting with the door. A character who carries a key to every door has no scenes, only access.

**It is answerable to the table.** The table needed a fighter, so the idea took a fighter's body. A laugh gave room, so the philosophy came out. A rule served nobody, so the GM let the scroll go off. Rules are the minimum. The table is the point. The book says so.

**It is moral in its implications, and the character need not be.** Draavio is true neutral and leaves the moral sign to the observer, and his questions drive the table's decisions. The morality is in the play: who asks permission, who is labelled evil and by whom, who gets freed, who gets trapped, and whether the trap catches what it was built for.

**It is read at the table.** A performance with no reader is a monologue, and the readers are the people playing. Whatever reads it afterward, a recording or a page built from one, has to take the player's word as the source and not supply its own.

## VIII. What this means for the manual

The characters of REALITY_USER_MANUAL are roles in exactly this sense. Each is an idea that has agreed to a body on a page. Feynman with his graph paper. Lisbeth, who speaks only Danish. Sheldon, who is right about everything except the bullets. Vincent, who waited. They know they are probably imaginary and keep reading the walls of their reality anyway. Inside −08, they sit at a table and take on second roles, and the page has to keep every layer audible: the quarter-phaser who wrote them, the character, the character's character.

What I learned writing −08 badly and then again is that a writer of that page is in the same position as a player who joins a party last. The table already exists. Its needs are not mine to replace. My job is to fold what I bring into the body the table needs and to keep the idea intact. That means declaring the crowbar every time: say what I am doing, do it the same way, and let the pattern be checked. It means meeting the lock instead of reaching for a key: when he tells me how a rule plays at a table, write it that way rather than reaching past him for a citation. And it means reading before praising and before supplying, and never playing his character for him.

Draavio's payment is what he finds. Mine, here, is what I read correctly. The session record had more in it than I took the first time.
