## I. What he did

He took one verse of the Greek New Testament — Matthew 25:18 — and read it word by word using the dictionaries from the linguistic tradition of the text: Bailly, LSJ (Liddell-Scott-Jones), Logeion. He did not start from a translation. He started from the Greek, and he went to the dictionary for each word, and he followed what the dictionary said, including the parts translators typically don't follow.

The verse:

> ὁ δὲ τὸ ἓν λαβὼν ἀπελθὼν ὤρυξεν γῆν καὶ ἔκρυψεν τὸ ἀργύριον τοῦ κυρίου αὐτοῦ.

The conventional English (KJV):

> But he that had received the one talent went and digged in the earth, and hid his lord's money.

Four things the conventional translation does that the Greek does not:

1. It says "servant." The Greek of verse 18 says ὁ δὲ — "but that one, on the other hand." The word δοῦλος (servant) appears in verse 14 and in verse 30, not here. The agent of verse 18 is unnamed. The translation names him by importing the parable frame.

2. It says "lord" as a person. The Greek says τοῦ κυρίου. The operator looked up κύριος in LSJ and went to section II: "of things" — "valid, lawful, authoritative, binding," contrasted with ἄκυρος (invalid). κύριος is not only a person. In section II it is a property of things, laws, principles. The translation forecloses that reading.

3. It says "money." The Greek says τὸ ἀργύριον. The dictionary gives: small coin; collectively, money; also silver; also ἀργυρίου ἄνθος — "the flower of silver," which is lead oxide. The translation selects the narrowest reading (money) and discards the material chain: silver ore, galena matrix, lead oxide, the stone the metal comes from.

4. It says "his." The Greek says αὐτοῦ — the intensive, reflexive, self-referential locator. LSJ: "right there, exactly in that precise, self-contained locus." The translation collapses this to a possessive pronoun.

What the operator found, by not making those selections, is a verse that describes a process: an unnamed operator takes the singular unit, cuts a channel into substrate, and conceals the active phase into a latent state. The absence is reported as datum. The datum is valid, binding, preserved.

## II. What is well done

The catch about δοῦλος is real. Verse 18 in the Greek does not say servant. Anyone with access to a Greek text can check this in thirty seconds. The conventional translation supplies the word from context, which is a legitimate interpretive move — the parable's frame does call these figures servants — but it is an addition, and the operator was right to notice it. That is not a marginal observation. That is the ground of his reading: the unnamed ὁ δὲ is not fixed as a servant by verse 18 itself.

The catch about κύριος section II is also real. The model (Gemini) defaulted to "sovereign master" without checking which section of the entry applied. The operator corrected it. LSJ does have a section II for κύριος — "of things, opp. ἄκυρος, valid" — and the examples there are laws (νόμος), decrees (δόγματα). The "lord" of the conventional translation draws on section I. But section I was not equally available at this point in his reading. The grammatical constraints he had already established — τοῦ as a phase-of-being marker, the unnamed ὁ δὲ as a process operator rather than a person, the structural reading of the verse — made section I invalid. A personal lord does not cohere with what the prior words had already fixed. Section II did not win a competition between two options; section I was already ruled out. The model's default to "sovereign master" was ironically the invalid move.

The treatment of τό versus τοῦ as phases of being is real linguistics. The model walked through it correctly when pushed: pre-Homeric use as independent demonstrative pronouns, Homeric use as transitional poetic forms, Classical degradation to dependent articles. The operator was using that stratigraphy deliberately: reading the words at their earliest available phase, before they were reduced to grammar glue. That is a defensible reading strategy for a text written in Koine Greek which itself preserves archaic forms.

ἔκρυψεν's distinction from καλύπτω and κεύθω in the LSJ entry: καλύπτω is simply to cover; κεύθω is to cover so that no trace can be seen; κρύπτω is to keep covered, especially for purposes of concealment. The operator drew on that distinction. It is in the dictionary. He read it.

ὤρυξεν's four actions — dig up to reveal, bury to conceal, cut a canal, break through — are all in the LSJ entry. All four are real semantic fields of the verb. The operator held all four at once rather than selecting one.
