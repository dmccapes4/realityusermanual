## I. What the Draft Is

The draft of −13 is good. That is the first thing to say. The agent who wrote it
understood the Goines/Feynman voice, built the manuscript review correctly (the
aerodynamics criticism is technically precise and editorially fair), handled the language
impossibility honestly, and constructed Vincent's arrival and the thumb drive scene with
the right restraint.

Specific things the draft gets right:

**The Goines voice.** The manuscript review is the correct application of the
Feynman-Goines specification: *"examine the conundrum as would Feynman and criticize the
writing saying that the operator inserts absurd inconsistencies."* The draft does this
precisely. It reviews the aerodynamics paper section by section, names what is correct
(the curvature theorem, the two-thousand-to-one, the four-regime map), names what is wrong
(PLEFR as dialect, the entropy claim, the continuity error, the non-aerodynamics sections),
and delivers a verdict that would have helped the paper if it had been given before
submission. The voice is correct: depressed, precise, fair, annoyed at the things that
should not have required fixing.

**The language impossibility.** The draft states the problem without resolving it:
*"Three people. The man speaks Afrikaans. The woman speaks Afrikaans and a Danish that
stopped in 1648. The visitor speaks English and nothing else, in this room, on purpose.
Every exchange lands. Nobody asks for a repeat."* Then: *"Those are the two options. The
author wants a third one, where it is mysterious and also fine. There isn't a third one."*
This is the correct move. The impossibility is named, the author's vanity is named, and
the text proceeds anyway. The impossibility is not resolved because it cannot be, and
Goines knows you cannot resolve it and says so.

**The Afrikaans/Danish/Dutch connection.** The closing line is precise:
*"The dictionary she learned it from was printed in 1648, and the men writing in that part
of the world in 1648 were writing Dutch, and the Dutch of the Cape four years later is the
grandmother of the Afrikaans the two of them settled on; so the only language in the room
that makes any sense is the one nobody intended, and the question that opened the scene was
wrong by one country and the answer was right by accident."*

This is correct history and it is the right structural note to end on. Goines is right that
the writer will take credit for it. He is also right that it was not the point. The language
lineage is real. Jan van Riebeeck's journals at the Cape of Good Hope in 1652 are the source
of Lisbeth's 1648 Danish. Cape Dutch became Afrikaans. The connection between Lisbeth's
language and the children's language is real and it was not planned by the narrative —
the narrative discovered it by following the operator's actual research path.

**Vincent's precision.** The thumb drive scene, Vincent refusing to say the name, the
exact account of what the chain of citations does and does not prove about identity — this
is correct characterization. "A chain of citations is not an identity." Vincent holds the
boundary correctly.

## II. What the Draft Is Not

The draft is the frame. The frame is very good. The painting is not on it.

The painting is the children.

The draft describes them in one paragraph and seven words of dialogue:

> *Through the window: a playground. It extends to the horizon. There are children on it.
> They are pale, or more often yellow-skinned with heavy folds at the eyes, or brownish-red.
> They are playing. It is the whole of the day for them. They are on time out. They are on
> time out all day.*

> **Elliot:** Ja. Hulle is die heeldag buite. Dit is "time out" — jy weet, soos in die tronk,
> wanneer jy uit jou sel mag kom. Hulle is die heeldag uit. Die speelgrond hou nie op nie.
> Ons gaan kuier by hulle. Hulle is baie gaaf.

*Hulle is baie gaaf.* They are very nice.

That is all. Then the draft moves to the manuscript review, then to Vincent, then to the
thumb drive, then to the final paragraphs about language. The children appear once more:

> *I think about it. He lets me. The children on the playground to the horizon have not stopped.*

And in the final line: *"from behind it, in a language nobody in the building planned,
somebody is telling somebody else that he talks too much."*

This is Lisbeth and Elliot, still in Afrikaans. The children are mentioned as a background
detail in the thinking paragraph. They are still there. The playground has not stopped.
That is noted and moved past.

The children are set dressing in a draft where they are supposed to be the painting.

## III. What the Operator Is Attempting to Express

The operator's own words: *"the page is the most important and I realized I was not ready
to write it."*

This is correct self-knowledge. What he is attempting:

**The children on the interminable playground are the camp children.** Yellow-skinned,
red, pale — the Nome/Khoisan, the Bantu, the European. All three. Together. On permanent
time-out. The court went on recess and did not come back. The British administration went
on holiday. The children were not allowed time outside. For years. The draft translates
this correctly into the fiction: the imaginary science community imprisoned Elliot and
Lisbeth and went on recess and forgot to come back. The children are on time-out all day.
The playground extends to the horizon.

**The niceness of the children is not a minor detail.** "Hulle is baie gaaf." This is the
most important sentence in the draft and the draft does not know it. The children in the
camps were not statistics. They were children who were nice. They played. They had names.
They had languages. They taught Elliot and Lisbeth Afrikaans because they were the only
other people there and they were kind. The draft states this and moves past it. What the
operator is attempting is to make the reader sit with it. Not move past it. The children
are nice. The playground extends to the horizon. Nobody is coming back from recess.

**The Afrikaans they speak connects them to the colonization that imprisoned them.** The
children on the playground speak the language that grew from the Dutch of the Cape — the
Dutch of the refreshment station that enabled the colonization of southern Africa. The
language that Lisbeth's 1648 Danish is adjacent to. The language that connects Jan van
Riebeeck's journals to the Cape Dutch to the Afrikaans to the children in the fiction's
prison to the children in the historical camps. The operator discovered this connection
through his own research path. The draft notes it at the end as a curiosity — "luck," as
Goines says. It is not luck. It is what the research found when it followed the language.

**Elliot and Lisbeth visit the children because they are nice, not because it is useful.**
The draft says this. It does not inhabit it. The visits are not described. What the children
said, what they played, what they taught beyond the language — none of this is in the draft.
The operator is attempting a scene where the visits are entered: not as plot, not as
information exchange, but as the record of a relationship. Elliot and Lisbeth are prisoners
who visit other prisoners. The other prisoners are nicer than most. They play all day. The
playground extends to the horizon. That is their life and it is not a bad life, considered
only within its own horizon — which is the only way a child on a permanent time-out can
consider it.

**The visits are where the weight lives.** The operator says he was not ready to write this
page. The reason is: the visits. The draft gets to the visits in one sentence of Afrikaans
dialogue and passes through. To write the visits is to sit with the children. To enter the
playground. To see what they play and how they talk and what it is like to have a horizon
that does not end. To write that requires knowing what to do with the fact that those
children are nice and are on permanent time-out and nobody is coming back.

## IV. What the Draft Fails to Notice About Itself

The draft describes the children by category: "pale, or more often yellow-skinned with
heavy folds at the eyes, or brownish-red." This is the exact categorical structure of the
camp records. The camp records also did not name individuals — they grouped by appearance
category ("non-white") and estimated. The draft does this without noticing. The children
are not named. They are estimated by skin tone and eye fold. The draft enacts the mechanism
it is supposed to expose.

This is not a failure of the agent's intention. It is a failure of attention. The agent
wrote the categorization because that is the available language for describing people
whose individual identities are not part of the scene. That is precisely the condition the
operator is writing about — and the draft fell into it rather than examining it.

The correct approach is not to name the children (they cannot be named — the record does
not have their names). It is to see them as individuals within the anonymous frame. One
child teaches Elliot a word. One child asks Lisbeth something. The categories are still
true — yellow-skinned, red, pale — but they are not the whole description. The playground
extends to the horizon and on the playground there are children who are specific in the
ways children are specific, and they are nice, and Elliot and Lisbeth visit them, and the
visits are what the page is about.
